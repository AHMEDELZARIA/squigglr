\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

This document is the Hazard Analysis (HA) of \progname{}, a website that
children use on a touchscreen device with a stylus to practise drawing,
and that supporting adults use to choose that practice and follow its
records. The HA asks how \progname{} could contribute to harm, and turns
the answers into safety requirements recorded in the Software
Requirements Specification (SRS). Items from the SRS are cited by their
identifiers (IDs) as plain text: goals G.1 to G.6, constraints CON-\#\#\#,
facts FACT-\#\#\#, business rules BR-\#\#\#, assumptions ASM-\#\#\#,
requirement sources SRC-\#\#\#, personas PER-1 to PER-3, open issues
OI-\#\#, likely changes LC-\#\#\# and use cases UC-\#\#\#. The subsections
below give the background, the purpose and scope of this document, the
definition of hazard it uses, the changes made to the course template, and
a roadmap of the document.

\subsection{Background}

\progname{} supports the visual-motor practice of kindergarten and primary
school learners, and of older children who need continued support,
including some children with autism or attention-deficit/hyperactivity
disorder (ADHD) (SRC-001). Its core, set by goals G.1 to G.6, has four
parts: guided drawing lessons whose stages give gradually less help, from
a demonstration through tracing a full outline and drawing between
endpoints to drawing with no guide; a creative space where \progname{}
refines a drawing a little, keeps it editable and lets the child restore
the original or, if the child cannot, an adult (BR-005); a record of every
attempt; and
progress summaries for supporting adults. Supporting adults are parents,
guardians, caregivers, educators and learning-support staff. They hold the
accounts, choose lessons and starting stages, and decide when a child
moves on (BR-001, BR-003, CON-007). \progname{} gives no diagnosis, no
judgement of a child's abilities and no comparison with other children
(BR-006).

Several conditions of use shape \progname{}'s hazards. Children may
practise without an adult watching (ASM-002), may not be able to read
(FACT-007), and may share one device with other children (ASM-008). Adults
decide on a child's practice from the records (ASM-006), so a record that
misstates what the child did can mislead them (BR-004). Creative drawings
may be refined with an existing pretrained model that the team does not
train or run (Development Plan, SRC-002; LC-004). The website also depends
on hosting, sign-in and network services outside the team's control and,
at school, on information technology (IT) staff who manage the devices
and networks (PER-3, ASM-013).

\subsection{Purpose and Scope of this Document}

This document records the hazards the team has identified, the failure
modes behind them, and the action recommended for each. Its outputs are
safety requirements: every recommended action traces to a requirement in
the SRS, and the requirements derived from this analysis are listed again
in Section~\ref{sec:safety-reqs}. The team uses the analysis to decide
what the SRS must require and what later verification must test; reviewers
use it to check that the requirements address the ways \progname{} can
fail. The analysis covers \progname{} as the SRS specifies it, used at
home, at school and in other places where children practise.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} states the losses considered and what lies outside
the scope, and Section~\ref{sec:boundary} draws the system boundary.

\subsection{Definition of Hazard}

This analysis uses the definition of hazard from Nancy Leveson's work that
the course adopts \citep{Smith2026L08}. In the wording of
\citet[p.~17]{LevesonAndThomas2018}, a hazard is ``a system state or set
of conditions that, together with a particular set of worst-case
environmental conditions, will lead to a loss''. A loss ``involves
something of value to stakeholders'' \citep[p.~16]{LevesonAndThomas2018}.
For \progname{}, the system is the set of components inside the boundary
of Section~\ref{sec:boundary}, and the environment is everything outside
it: the child, the supporting adult, the device, the network and the
outside services. For example, a learner profile left active after one
child's turn on a shared school tablet is a hazard. When the next child
draws without an adult noticing (the environmental condition), that
child's attempts are saved to the wrong record, and an adult later reads a
history that mixes two children (a loss of privacy and of correct
records).

\progname{} controls no physical equipment, so the course's ``weakened''
notion of harm applies: besides injury, harm includes security failures,
insensitivity to users, frustrating software and poor data quality
\citep{Smith2026L08}. Section~\ref{sec:scope} turns this into five losses
specific to \progname{}. Safety is also not the same as correctness
\citep{Smith2026L08}: a component can meet its specification and still
contribute to a loss in a harmful situation, so the analysis looks at
conditions of use as well as defects.

\subsection{Changes to the Template}

The course template has been changed as follows. Every template section is
kept, in its original order.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Front matter:} a List of Figures and a List of Tables follow
  the Table of Contents, and the template's guidance notes are removed.
  \item \emph{Introduction:} divided into background, purpose and scope,
  the definition of hazard, these changes and a document roadmap.
  \item \emph{Scope and purpose (Section~\ref{sec:scope}):} the losses are
  listed with IDs L1 to L5, and each item left out of scope is given a
  reason and a stance on its hazards.
  \item \emph{System boundaries and components
  (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}):} the components have IDs C1 to C8 and fixed
  names, which the Component column of the H-MUH table and the coverage
  table of the Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA) repeat exactly. A
  figure shows the boundary, and a table states
  how failures outside it enter the analysis. C8 is added to the seven
  components first planned, for the reason given in
  Section~\ref{sec:boundary}.
  \item \emph{FMEA (Section~\ref{sec:fmea}):} each table has the columns
  Hazard ID, Component, Failure mode, Cause(s), Effect and loss, Category,
  Recommended action and Mitigating requirement IDs. The Category column is
  added; it classes each row as Physical, Psychological/well-being,
  Privacy/security, Data integrity or Availability. Each team member's
  rows form a subsection with a table of their own, so each hazard ID
  carries its writer's prefix: Table~\ref{tab:fmea-ray} (H-RAY),
  Table~\ref{tab:fmea-ahm} (H-AHM) and the H-MUH table.
  True duplicates are combined by reference to one row. A coverage table
  then checks every component of Table~\ref{tab:components} against every
  category, across all the tables. Each recommended action begins with
  Eliminate, Mitigate or Document/Test. Rows are not ranked or filtered by
  a risk priority number, following the course's advice against
  prioritising hazards by probability \citep{Smith2026L08}.
  \item \emph{Safety and security requirements
  (Section~\ref{sec:safety-reqs}):} as the teaching assistant (TA) advised
  (SRC-006), each safety requirement is written once, in the SRS, and the
  FMEA refers to it by ID. This section repeats the statement of every
  requirement derived from the HA exactly as the SRS states it, with the
  hazards it mitigates.
  \item \emph{Roadmap (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}):} each requirement
  derived from the HA is assigned to a project phase, PH-1 (proof of
  concept), PH-2 (Revision~0) or PH-3 (final), or to Future, with the phase
  dates set in the SRS Introduction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Purpose of Hazard Analysis}\label{sec:scope}

The purpose of this analysis is to find the ways \progname{}'s components
can fail, or can work as specified yet still contribute to harm in some
situation, and to recommend for each an action that eliminates the
hazard, mitigates it, or documents and tests it \citep{Smith2026L08}. The
actions become requirements in the SRS, so the analysis is part of
specifying \progname{}, not a check made after it is built. The analysis
covers \progname{} as the SRS specifies it for goals G.1 to G.6, used by
children and supporting adults wherever children practise: at home, in
classrooms, and in places such as rehabilitation centres (SRS, Anticipated
Workplace Environment). Hazards are identified per component, so the scope
of the analysis is the system boundary of Section~\ref{sec:boundary}.

\subsection{Out of Scope}

The items below are outside the scope of this analysis. Losses or items
that are excluded should be documented
\citep[p.~17]{LevesonAndThomas2018}, so each item has a reason and a stance
on what happens to its hazards.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Camera-based finger tracing.} A stretch goal (SRC-001). The
  specialists stated that tracing in the air practises gross rather than
  fine motor skills and should come after the touchscreen activities
  (FACT-006), and no core requirement uses a camera. \emph{Stance:} a
  camera would capture images of the child, while a learner profile holds
  only a first name and a non-identifying username (BR-001). Before camera
  tracing is added, this analysis must gain a camera component and analyse
  its privacy hazards; nothing here covers them.
  \item \textbf{Letter tracing and drawing.} A stretch goal (SRC-001); two
  specialists saw letters as a later stage after shapes (ASM-010;
  SRC-009.04). \emph{Stance:}
  letters would be new lessons in the same catalogue (LC-001), so the
  hazards of the canvas, lesson and storage components apply to them
  unchanged. Letter-specific hazards, such as an adult reading letter
  attempts as a handwriting assessment (BR-006), must be added when letters
  are specified.
  \item \textbf{Finger input as a supported input.} A stretch goal (CON-003,
  LC-006); stage checks and value ranges are set for stylus input
  (ASM-003). \emph{Stance:} drawing with a finger on a device without a
  stylus remains possible, and is in scope as a condition of the device
  outside the boundary, since such attempts may be judged wrongly
  (ASM-003). Only finger input as a supported feature, with its own ranges,
  is out of scope; adding it requires the canvas and lesson rows to be
  reviewed.
  \item \textbf{Offline use.} Out of the initial scope (ASM-005).
  \emph{Stance:} a slow or lost connection is in scope as a condition of the
  network outside the boundary. Only an offline mode, in which \progname{}
  works without a connection, is out of scope.
  \item \textbf{Internal operation of outside equipment and services.} The
  device, browser, network, hosting service, authentication service and
  refinement model are not analysed internally, because the team neither
  builds nor controls them. Physical harm from the device itself, such as
  a dropped tablet, stays with the adult nearby (ASM-002).
  \emph{Stance:} their failures are in scope at the boundary, as causes of
  failure modes of the components that use them
  (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}).
  \item \textbf{The project's own activities.} Requirements gathering and
  evaluation involve no children (CON-005, CON-006). These are constraints
  on the project, not behaviour of \progname{}. \emph{Stance:} any future
  study with children needs ethics approval first (FACT-008), which this
  analysis does not replace.
\end{itemize}
Diagnosis and treatment are never features of \progname{} (BR-006), so no
hazard of a diagnostic function is analysed. The use of practice records as
diagnostic evidence, however, is in scope, because the records exist and
adults may show them to others (loss L5; H-RAY-003).

\section{Critical Assumptions}\label{sec:assumptions}

The assumptions below are kept few and are written so that they do not
remove hazards. Squigglr does not assume that an adult watches the child
during practice, that the network is always available, or that the device
meets Squigglr's requirements; those conditions are treated as failure
modes in the Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA). Sources are cited by
their identifiers in the Software Requirements Specification (SRS),
Appendix A, and interview turns by speaker and timestamp, for example
(SRC-005, P1, 34:13). Each assumption lists the H-RAY hazards it affects.

\begin{description}
\item[CA-001] An adult sets up Squigglr for a child, chooses and starts
the lesson and its starting stage, and is the only person meant to accept
a suggestion to go back a stage. Within a lesson the child does not
navigate, exit or restart; Squigglr moves between stages. An adult may not
be present or watching while the child practises.\\
\emph{Rationale:} children should practise without constant adult help,
while adults handle setup (SRC-005, P1, 34:13), and adults decide progression
(SRC-005, P1, 28:37; CON-007).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-001, H-RAY-004, H-RAY-005.

\item[CA-002] The device, browser, touchscreen or stylus in use may not be
one of the supported combinations (\texttt{SUPPORTED\_DEVICES}, TBD), and
Squigglr may be opened on a device without a stylus, where the child can
draw with a finger but not as intended (ASM-003).\\
\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr is a website opened from a web address with no
installation (CON-001, CON-002), so the team does not control the device.
The supported combinations are not yet defined (CON-003), and the team did
not promise that Squigglr works on every device (SRC-005, Moustafa,
6:03).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-002.

\item[CA-003] The network connection may be slow or lost at any time,
including during an attempt.\\
\emph{Rationale:} attempts are saved to an account and reviewed later from
another device, and offline access was not promised (SRC-005, Moustafa,
6:03). Home and school networks are outside the team's control.\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-002, H-RAY-006.

\item[CA-004] One device may be used by several children, at school or at
home.\\
\emph{Rationale:} school devices may be shared by several children
(SRC-004), and Squigglr may be used at home, at school or in other settings
such as rehabilitation centres (SRC-005, P2, 21:25).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-006.

\item[CA-005] The adults who read practice records are not assumed to have
training in visual-motor development, and they may show the records to
other people, such as teachers or therapists.\\
\emph{Rationale:} parents and caregivers are not assumed to be specialists
(SRS, Hands-On Users), and records exist to support conversations about
what a child needs (SRC-005, Moustafa, 6:03). Squigglr makes no diagnosis
or treatment claims (SRC-001).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-003.

\item[CA-006] The supporting adult chooses the adult-set values, such as
\texttt{STAGE\_PASS}, \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}, for each child within a range the team sets
(SRC-008). The ranges are not found from child testing, and the value an
adult picks, or the default, may not suit their child.\\
\emph{Rationale:} no information is gathered from children and no study
with children is run (CON-005, CON-006), so the ranges cannot be tuned on
children (SRS, Goals of the Project). Children reach these skills at
different points (SRC-005, P1, 20:27).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-004, H-RAY-005, H-RAY-007.
\end{description}

\begin{enumerate}

\item What went well while writing this deliverable?

\textbf{Rayan:} Identifying the hazards was easier than expected because we
already had good sources to work from. The transcript of our interview with
three domain specialists on October 1, 2026 gave us concrete statements, with
timestamps, about how the intended children use apps and what could go wrong
for them. For example, the specialists said adults should decide progression
rather than the app, that audio feedback should be possible to mute, and that
Squigglr may be used at home, at school or in other settings. Each of these
pointed directly to a failure mode we could analyse, so we were not guessing at
hazards from scratch. Our meeting with our supervisor, Dr.~Anand, on October
14, 2026 helped in the same way: once Dr.~Anand explained that thresholds
should be set by the adult for each child within a range, the hazards around
wrong or extreme threshold values became clear. Because the SRS already
recorded these sources, business rules and assumptions, we could trace each
hazard and critical assumption back to a specific statement, which also made
the mitigations easier to justify.

\item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
did you resolve them?

\textbf{Rayan:} The main pain point was that my hazards kept changing as other
parts of the documents changed. I wrote my first set of hazards and critical
assumptions based on the SRS as it stood at the time, where the team would set
fixed values for thresholds such as \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}. In our meeting on October 14, 2026, Dr.~Anand advised
that suitable values differ from child to child, so I changed those thresholds
to be set by the supporting adult within a range the team defines. When I
reviewed the hazard analysis again, I found that this changed the hazards: the
risk was no longer only a wrong team-chosen value, but also an adult choosing a
value at the edge of the range, so I added a new hazard and safety requirement
for it. The same thing happened when I added the improvement rule, which can
extend a lesson while a child is improving, and when we confirmed that the
child does not navigate inside a lesson. Each time, I had to go back and update
the affected hazards, critical assumptions and mitigations so they matched the
SRS. It was time-consuming, but it showed me how closely the hazard analysis
depends on the requirements, and that it has to be reviewed again whenever the
SRS changes.

\item Which of your listed risks had your team thought of before this
deliverable, and which did you think of while doing this deliverable? For
the latter ones (ones you thought of while doing the Hazard Analysis), how
did they come about?

\textbf{Rayan:} Before this deliverable, the team had already thought of two
of my risks. Because Squigglr runs in a browser, we expected that attempts
could be lost or not saved on an unreliable browser or device (H-RAY-002). We
also knew that adults might read a child's records as a diagnosis (H-RAY-003),
which is why the problem statement already avoids any diagnostic claims.

The other five risks I only thought of while doing the hazard analysis. They
came about because this was the first time I had to work out the requirements
for the application in detail, while writing the SRS alongside this document.
Most of them came from the critical assumptions, and several of those
assumptions came from our interview with the specialists on October 1, 2026.
The specialists said children can use the app through images and icons while
adults handle setup, which led to the assumption that an adult sets up each
session. Asking what happens if no adult is actually watching led to the risk
of a child reaching adult-only actions (H-RAY-001), and, together with defining
what happens after repeated failed attempts, to the risks of a child becoming
distressed (H-RAY-004) and of a lesson being restarted straight after it ends
(H-RAY-005). The specialists also said Squigglr may be used at home, at school
or in other settings, which led to the assumption that a device may be shared
and to the risk of a child's records being saved under the wrong profile
(H-RAY-006). The last risk came from our meeting on October 14, 2026, where
Dr.~Anand advised that adults should set the thresholds for each child; this
raised the risk of a threshold being set to a value that does not suit the
child (H-RAY-007).

Looking back, most of my risks only became visible once the requirements were
concrete enough to question. The risks we knew about beforehand were general
ones that apply to almost any browser app, while the ones I found during this
deliverable were specific to how children and adults will actually use
Squigglr. This showed me that the hazard analysis cannot be done well in
isolation; it depends on knowing the requirements and on hearing from people
who understand the users.

\item Other than the risk of physical harm (some projects may not have any
appreciable risks of this form), list at least 2 other types of risk in
software products. Why are they important to consider?

\textbf{Rayan:} Most software carries risks that have nothing to do with
physical harm. One is privacy and security. Almost every application stores
some information about its users, and if that information is exposed, accessed
by the wrong person or mixed up between users, it can harm those users and
destroy their trust in the product. This matters even more when the users are
vulnerable. In Squigglr, for example, the records describe a child's
difficulties, so they must only be visible to the adults responsible for that
child. A second is data integrity and availability. People make decisions based
on what software shows them, so data that is lost, corrupted or shown
incorrectly can lead them to the wrong decision, even when nothing visibly
crashes. In our case, an adult choosing a child's next lesson relies on the
attempt records being complete and accurate. A third is psychological harm.
Software that frustrates, confuses or misleads its users can affect their
well-being or lead them to wrong conclusions, for example by implying something
about a person that the software cannot actually judge. For Squigglr, that
means avoiding repeated failure without a break and making sure progress
summaries are never read as a diagnosis. These risks are important to consider
because they are easy to overlook when there is no obvious physical danger, yet
they can cause real harm to users and to the people who rely on the software.
