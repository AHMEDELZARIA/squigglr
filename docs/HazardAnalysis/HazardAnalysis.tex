\section{Critical Assumptions}

The assumptions below are kept few and are written so that they do not
remove hazards. Squigglr does not assume that an adult watches the child
during practice, that the network is always available, or that the device
meets Squigglr's requirements; those conditions are treated as failure
modes in the Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA). Sources are cited by
their identifiers in the Software Requirements Specification (SRS),
Appendix A, and interview turns by speaker and timestamp, for example
(SRC-005, P1, 34:13). Each assumption lists the H-RAY hazards it affects.

\begin{description}
\item[CA-001] An adult sets up Squigglr for a child, chooses and starts
the lesson and its starting stage, and is the only person meant to accept
a suggestion to go back a stage. Within a lesson the child does not
navigate, exit or restart; Squigglr moves between stages. An adult may not
be present or watching while the child practises.\\
\emph{Rationale:} children should practise without constant adult help,
while adults handle setup (SRC-005, P1, 34:13), and adults decide progression
(SRC-005, P1, 28:37; CON-007).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-001, H-RAY-004, H-RAY-005.

\item[CA-002] The device, browser, touchscreen or stylus in use may not be
one of the supported combinations (\texttt{SUPPORTED\_DEVICES}, TBD), and
Squigglr may be opened on a device without a stylus, where the child can
draw with a finger but not as intended (ASM-003).\\
\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr is a website opened from a web address with no
installation (CON-001, CON-002), so the team does not control the device.
The supported combinations are not yet defined (CON-003), and the team did
not promise that Squigglr works on every device (SRC-005, Moustafa,
6:03).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-002.

\item[CA-003] The network connection may be slow or lost at any time,
including during an attempt.\\
\emph{Rationale:} attempts are saved to an account and reviewed later from
another device, and offline access was not promised (SRC-005, Moustafa,
6:03). Home and school networks are outside the team's control.\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-002, H-RAY-006.

\item[CA-004] One device may be used by several children, at school or at
home.\\
\emph{Rationale:} school devices may be shared by several children
(SRC-004), and Squigglr may be used at home, at school or in other settings
such as rehabilitation centres (SRC-005, P2, 21:25).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-006.

\item[CA-005] The adults who read practice records are not assumed to have
training in visual-motor development, and they may show the records to
other people, such as teachers or therapists.\\
\emph{Rationale:} parents and caregivers are not assumed to be specialists
(SRS, Hands-On Users), and records exist to support conversations about
what a child needs (SRC-005, Moustafa, 6:03). Squigglr makes no diagnosis
or treatment claims (SRC-001).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-003.

\item[CA-006] The supporting adult chooses the adult-set values, such as
\texttt{STAGE\_PASS}, \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}, for each child within a range the team sets
(SRC-008). The ranges are not found from child testing, and the value an
adult picks, or the default, may not suit their child.\\
\emph{Rationale:} no information is gathered from children and no study
with children is run (CON-005, CON-006), so the ranges cannot be tuned on
children (SRS, Goals of the Project). Children reach these skills at
different points (SRC-005, P1, 20:27).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-004, H-RAY-005, H-RAY-007.
\end{description}

\section{Failure Mode and Effect Analysis}

This section applies Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA) to the
components of \progname{}. Subsection~\ref{sec:fmea-method} states how the
analysis was carried out. Each team member's rows are in a table of their
own: Table~\ref{tab:fmea-ray} (H-RAY), Table~\ref{tab:fmea-ahm} (H-AHM)
and Table~\ref{tab:fmea-muh} (H-MUH). Table~\ref{tab:fmea-coverage} places
every row of the three tables under a component and checks that every
component has been examined for every category of harm. Each row ends with
the requirements of the Software Requirements Specification (SRS) that
mitigate it; the Safety and Security Requirements section lists the
requirements derived from this analysis again.

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:fmea-method}

FMEA is a bottom-up method: it starts from the functions of each component
and asks how each can fail \citep{Smith2026L08}. The components are the
functional components named in the System Boundaries and Components
section, not modules of a design. The analysis uses the course's
definitions \citep{Smith2026L08}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{failure} is a departure of a component from its required or
  intended function;
  \item a \emph{failure mode} is the manner in which the failure is
  observed by a child, an adult or a tester;
  \item an \emph{effect} is the consequence of a failure mode; each row
  names the losses it leads to, and the H-MUH rows use the IDs L1 to L5 of
  the Scope and Purpose section;
  \item a \emph{cause} is a reason the failure mode occurs, including
  conditions outside the boundary, such as a lost connection (CA-003) or a
  device shared by several children (CA-004);
  \item a \emph{recommended action} says what is done to eliminate or
  mitigate the hazard, and becomes a safety requirement.
\end{itemize}

Failure modes were found in three steps. First, the functions of each
component were taken from its role in the System Boundaries and Components
section, and four guide words were applied to each function: it is not
performed; it is performed wrongly or only in part; it is performed at the
wrong time or out of order; and it is performed when it should not be.
Second, every component was checked against the five categories of harm
(Physical, Psychological/well-being, Privacy/security, Data integrity and
Availability), so that a component that works as specified but still harms
a child is also recorded; safety is not the same as correctness
\citep{Smith2026L08}. For the creative refinement component, which uses an
external model, this check also covered the risks of artificial
intelligence (AI) that arise from how \progname{} uses the model:
compromise of privacy, over-reliance, loss of human agency, lack of
capability or robustness, and lack of transparency \citep{SlatteryEtAl2024}.
Third, each team member writes the rows for the parts of the SRS they own,
so each hazard ID carries its writer's prefix (H-RAY, H-MOU, H-AHM, H-MUH or
H-SHA), and each member's rows form a table of their own. The coverage
check of Subsection~\ref{sec:fmea-coverage} then places every row of every
table under one of the components, so that the rows of all members are
checked against the same components and categories.

Every row has a recommended action. Each action begins with
\emph{Eliminate} (remove the failure mode, for example by not offering a
function where it can cause the harm), \emph{Mitigate} (reduce its effect,
or make it visible and recoverable) or \emph{Document/Test} (a statement to
users or a verification activity that limits or checks what remains); one
cell may hold several. Actions state what \progname{} must do, not how. The
last column of each row names, by ID, the SRS requirements, goals or
constraints that carry out its action. For the H-MUH rows, each cited
requirement lists the hazard among its own traces, so the trace runs in
both directions. An action that needs a
requirement not yet in the SRS is added to the owner's section of the SRS,
or to its Safety-Critical Requirements subsection, before its ID appears in
the table.

Rows are not ranked, and no row is left out because its failure seems
unlikely. Many FMEAs rank rows by a risk priority number, the product of
ratings for severity, probability of occurrence and detection, and mitigate
only the rows above a threshold. The course advises against this, because
accidents have followed from judging that a failure is not likely to
happen, and there is seldom a good basis for the probabilities
\citep{Smith2026L08}. \progname{} has no usage data from children on which
to base them (CON-005, CON-006), so every row is mitigated.

Rows from different owners can describe the same failure. When two rows
share a failure mode and a cause, they are combined by reference: one row
keeps the shared causes and their mitigations, and the other states that it
is combined with that row, keeps only the causes that are its own, and
keeps its ID, so that no requirement's trace is broken. In Table~\ref{tab:fmea-muh}, rows are
grouped by component in the order of the components table of the System
Boundaries and Components section, and by ID within a group. Where a row's causes
also lie in another component, the coverage check of
Table~\ref{tab:fmea-coverage} records it under that component too.

\begin{enumerate}

\item What went well while writing this deliverable?

\textbf{Rayan:} Identifying the hazards was easier than expected because we
already had good sources to work from. The transcript of our interview with
three domain specialists on October 1, 2026 gave us concrete statements, with
timestamps, about how the intended children use apps and what could go wrong
for them. For example, the specialists said adults should decide progression
rather than the app, that audio feedback should be possible to mute, and that
Squigglr may be used at home, at school or in other settings. Each of these
pointed directly to a failure mode we could analyse, so we were not guessing at
hazards from scratch. Our meeting with our supervisor, Dr.~Anand, on October
14, 2026 helped in the same way: once Dr.~Anand explained that thresholds
should be set by the adult for each child within a range, the hazards around
wrong or extreme threshold values became clear. Because the SRS already
recorded these sources, business rules and assumptions, we could trace each
hazard and critical assumption back to a specific statement, which also made
the mitigations easier to justify.

\textbf{Ahmed:} What went well for me was having the learning activity
requirements written in enough detail that I could actually think
through how they might fail. I had been working on the guided-lesson
flow in the SRS, so I already knew the steps from drawing a stroke to
classifying an attempt, showing feedback and deciding whether the
learner advances. Looking at each of those steps made it much easier
to come up with specific hazards instead of just listing general
problems that any website could have.

It also helped that we were working on the SRS and Hazard Analysis at
the same time. When I identified a failure mode, I could check whether
an existing requirement already addressed it and point to that
requirement directly. For duplicate stylus events and incorrect
coordinate mapping, I could see that we needed two more explicit
safeguards in the SRS. I think that connection between the two
documents was the strongest part of my work, because the hazards led
to concrete requirements that we can eventually test.

\textbf{Moustafa:} What went well was how much this deliverable changed the
way I think about what we're building. When I think of developing a drawing
app for kids, the last thing that comes to mind is hazards and safety. But
going through my rows made me realize how many gaps we could have had if we
hadn't thought about this. For example, I never would have guessed a
drawing app could cause physical harm, but rapid flashing can trigger
seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy, which is why WCAG limits
flashing to three times a second. That became H-MOU-001 and SAF-MOU-003. I
think thinking in terms of hazards early on is going to be really useful
for my career in general, not just for this project.

\item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
did you resolve them?

\textbf{Rayan:} The main pain point was that my hazards kept changing as other
parts of the documents changed. I wrote my first set of hazards and critical
assumptions based on the SRS as it stood at the time, where the team would set
fixed values for thresholds such as \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}. In our meeting on October 14, 2026, Dr.~Anand advised
that suitable values differ from child to child, so I changed those thresholds
to be set by the supporting adult within a range the team defines. When I
reviewed the hazard analysis again, I found that this changed the hazards: the
risk was no longer only a wrong team-chosen value, but also an adult choosing a
value at the edge of the range, so I added a new hazard and safety requirement
for it. The same thing happened when I added the improvement rule, which can
extend a lesson while a child is improving, and when we confirmed that the
child does not navigate inside a lesson. Each time, I had to go back and update
the affected hazards, critical assumptions and mitigations so they matched the
SRS. It was time-consuming, but it showed me how closely the hazard analysis
depends on the requirements, and that it has to be reviewed again whenever the
SRS changes.

\textbf{Ahmed:} The hardest part was deciding where one hazard ended
and another began. A mistake in the drawing input can lead to a wrong
classification, which can then lead to incorrect feedback or an
inappropriate stage change. At first it was tempting to describe all
of those consequences in every row. I had to keep going back and
asking what the actual failure mode was, what caused it, and which
requirement would prevent or reduce it.

There was also some overlap with the hazards being written by other
team members. In particular, my initial feedback hazard was too close
to the work on confusing or discouraging feedback. After reviewing
that feedback, I narrowed my row to a technical problem: the system
choosing the wrong pre-approved message for the classification it
produced. This left the wording, tone and sensory aspects to the
other hazard owner. Another challenge was keeping the references
aligned as the SRS changed. Whenever we clarified a lesson rule or
added a safety requirement, I had to check the FMEA again rather than
assuming the old wording still worked. It took extra revisions, but
the result was more precise and easier to review.

\textbf{Moustafa:} The main pain point was that some of my hazards depended
on requirements nobody had written yet. For example, the hazards about
refinement changing a drawing too much and progress views misleading adults
belong with Muhammad's creative-drawing and progress-summary requirements,
which weren't written when I was doing my rows. I couldn't just leave the
mitigation column empty, so where a goal or business rule already covered
the hazard, I traced to that, and where nothing did, I wrote my own safety
requirements, SAF-MOU-001 and SAF-MOU-002. It also meant my rows came in
late, because I needed my own usability sections done first so the hazards
had something to point to.

\item Which of your listed risks had your team thought of before this
deliverable, and which did you think of while doing this deliverable? For
the latter ones (ones you thought of while doing the Hazard Analysis), how
did they come about?

\textbf{Rayan:} Before this deliverable, the team had already thought of two
of my risks. Because Squigglr runs in a browser, we expected that attempts
could be lost or not saved on an unreliable browser or device (H-RAY-002). We
also knew that adults might read a child's records as a diagnosis (H-RAY-003),
which is why the problem statement already avoids any diagnostic claims.

The other five risks I only thought of while doing the hazard analysis. They
came about because this was the first time I had to work out the requirements
for the application in detail, while writing the SRS alongside this document.
Most of them came from the critical assumptions, and several of those
assumptions came from our interview with the specialists on October 1, 2026.
The specialists said children can use the app through images and icons while
adults handle setup, which led to the assumption that an adult sets up each
session. Asking what happens if no adult is actually watching led to the risk
of a child reaching adult-only actions (H-RAY-001), and, together with defining
what happens after repeated failed attempts, to the risks of a child becoming
distressed (H-RAY-004) and of a lesson being restarted straight after it ends
(H-RAY-005). The specialists also said Squigglr may be used at home, at school
or in other settings, which led to the assumption that a device may be shared
and to the risk of a child's records being saved under the wrong profile
(H-RAY-006). The last risk came from our meeting on October 14, 2026, where
Dr.~Anand advised that adults should set the thresholds for each child; this
raised the risk of a threshold being set to a value that does not suit the
child (H-RAY-007).

Looking back, most of my risks only became visible once the requirements were
concrete enough to question. The risks we knew about beforehand were general
ones that apply to almost any browser app, while the ones I found during this
deliverable were specific to how children and adults will actually use
Squigglr. This showed me that the hazard analysis cannot be done well in
isolation; it depends on knowing the requirements and on hearing from people
who understand the users.

\textbf{Ahmed:} Before we started this deliverable, I had already
thought about the possibility of drawing input being incomplete and
of an incorrect attempt being allowed to pass. Those were concerns
that came up naturally while defining how a guided lesson should
evaluate strokes and move a learner to the next stage. They became
H-AHM-001 and H-AHM-004 once I described their causes, effects and
mitigations more systematically.

The FMEA made me consider several less obvious failures. For
H-AHM-002, I started thinking about what happens when input events are
reported more than once. Two overlapping strokes might be perfectly
intentional, but one event being duplicated by the software is a
different problem, and simply deleting repeated-looking lines would
not solve it. H-AHM-003 came from looking at how the canvas changes
size: even if the stylus reports valid points, a scaling or
coordinate-mapping mistake could shift the drawing before it is
scored. Those cases led to the new input-integrity safety requirements.

I also looked beyond the drawing itself. H-AHM-005 came from asking
whether a correct classification could still produce the wrong
feedback if the message lookup used the wrong stage or identifier.
H-AHM-006 came from tracing the failure streak, improvement rule and
adult-approval step together; an error there could suggest a stage
change at the wrong time or apply one without permission. Finally,
H-AHM-007 came from considering what happens when a learner's saved
settings are changed or profiles are switched. That exercise showed
me that a lot of meaningful risks are not obvious until you follow
the data and decisions across the entire workflow.

\textbf{Moustafa:} Most of my risks were things we had already thought
about before this deliverable, because we planned against them in the
presentation we gave the specialists on October 1. We had already decided
that feedback would highlight the unfinished part of a drawing instead of
failing the attempt or finishing it for the child, that there would be no
scores and fewer attempts wouldn't mean better skill, and that refinement
would only tidy the child's own drawing, with the original always
restorable. Those became H-MOU-002, H-MOU-004, H-MOU-005 and H-MOU-006. The
other two came up while doing this deliverable. Sensory overload
(H-MOU-001) came from the questionnaire, where one specialist warned about
excessive visual or auditory stimuli, and the flashing part came from WCAG
while I was writing the accessibility requirements. Confusing feedback
(H-MOU-003) came from the same work: once I let adults mute audio and said
feedback can't rely on colour alone, I started asking what happens to a
child who gets sound-only or colour-only feedback, or feedback they need
to read.

\item Other than the risk of physical harm (some projects may not have any
appreciable risks of this form), list at least 2 other types of risk in
software products. Why are they important to consider?

\textbf{Rayan:} Most software carries risks that have nothing to do with
physical harm. One is privacy and security. Almost every application stores
some information about its users, and if that information is exposed, accessed
by the wrong person or mixed up between users, it can harm those users and
destroy their trust in the product. This matters even more when the users are
vulnerable. In Squigglr, for example, the records describe a child's
difficulties, so they must only be visible to the adults responsible for that
child. A second is data integrity and availability. People make decisions based
on what software shows them, so data that is lost, corrupted or shown
incorrectly can lead them to the wrong decision, even when nothing visibly
crashes. In our case, an adult choosing a child's next lesson relies on the
attempt records being complete and accurate. A third is psychological harm.
Software that frustrates, confuses or misleads its users can affect their
well-being or lead them to wrong conclusions, for example by implying something
about a person that the software cannot actually judge. For Squigglr, that
means avoiding repeated failure without a break and making sure progress
summaries are never read as a diagnosis. These risks are important to consider
because they are easy to overlook when there is no obvious physical danger, yet
they can cause real harm to users and to the people who rely on the software.

\textbf{Ahmed:} One important type is data integrity risk. Software
can appear to work while quietly saving or interpreting the wrong
information. That matters for Squigglr because a drawing could be
captured incorrectly, marked as passing when it should not, or saved
under the wrong result. Adults rely on those records to understand
what happened during practice, so an inaccurate record is not just a
technical bug; it can influence their next decision.

Another type is privacy and security risk. A system may expose one
person's information to someone who should not have access to it, or
mix records between different users. Squigglr may be used on shared
devices, and its records concern children, so we need to think about
who can see each learner's history and how the active learner is
identified. Psychological or well-being risks also matter. Incorrect
feedback, repeated failures or unexpected progression can confuse or
frustrate a child even if there is no physical injury. These examples
reminded me that software safety is about more than preventing a crash
or a hardware failure. It also means making sure people can trust
what the system tells them and that it behaves in a way appropriate
for its users.


\textbf{Moustafa:} Two other types of risk are accessibility risk and legal
or compliance risk. Accessibility risk is when some people simply can't use
the software, and it's easy to miss because teams usually test on people
like themselves. For Squigglr, a child who can't read yet, is colour-blind
or has trouble with fine motor control could fail to use the app without
anyone noticing, which is the opposite of what we're building it for.
That's what most of my usability and accessibility requirements are there
to prevent. Legal and compliance risk is when software breaks a law or
regulation, which can get it pulled or fined even if it works fine. One of
our specialists pointed out that WCAG is adopted as law in many countries,
and Squigglr keeps records about children, so we have to think about both
accessibility and privacy rules before we can put it in front of real
families or schools.
