\subsection*{Rayan Nasrallah}

\textbf{1. What went well while writing this deliverable?}

Identifying the hazards was easier than expected because we already had good
sources to work from. The specialist interview transcript gave us concrete
statements, with timestamps, about how the intended children use apps and what
could go wrong for them. For example, the specialists said adults should decide
progression rather than the app, that audio feedback should be possible to
mute, and that Squigglr may be used at home, at school or in other settings.
Each of these pointed directly to a failure mode we could analyse, so we were
not guessing at hazards from scratch. The supervisor meeting helped in the same
way: once Dr.~Anand explained that thresholds should be set by the adult within
a range, the hazards around wrong or extreme threshold values became clear.
Because the SRS already recorded these sources, business rules and
assumptions, we could trace each hazard and critical assumption back to a
specific statement, which also made the mitigations easier to justify.

\textbf{2. What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
did you resolve them?}

The main pain point was that my hazards kept changing as other parts of the
documents changed. I wrote my first set of hazards and critical assumptions
based on the SRS as it stood at the time, where the team would set fixed values
for thresholds such as \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}.
After the supervisor meeting, I changed those thresholds to be set by the
supporting adult within a range the team defines. When I reviewed the hazard
analysis again, I found that this changed the hazards: the risk was no longer
only a wrong team-chosen value, but also an adult choosing a value at the edge
of the range, so I added a new hazard and safety requirement for it. The same
thing happened when I added the improvement rule, which can extend a lesson
while a child is improving, and when we confirmed that the child does not
navigate inside a lesson. Each time, I had to go back and update the affected
hazards, critical assumptions and mitigations so they matched the SRS. It was
time-consuming, but it showed me how closely the hazard analysis depends on the
requirements, and that it has to be reviewed again whenever the SRS changes.

\textbf{3. Which of your listed risks had your team thought of before this
deliverable, and which did you think of while doing this deliverable?}

Some risks were clear before I started. Because the app depends on the browser
and device it runs on, we already expected that attempts could be lost or not
saved on an unreliable browser or device (H-RAY-002). We also knew that adults
might read a child's records as a diagnosis (H-RAY-003), which is why the
problem statement already avoids any diagnostic claims. Most of the other risks
only came up while I was writing. Once I listed the critical assumptions, I
went through each one and asked what would happen if it failed. Assuming an
adult sets up and supervises each session led to the risk of a child reaching
adult-only actions when no adult is watching (H-RAY-001). Thinking about
repeated failed attempts led to the risks of a child becoming distressed
(H-RAY-004) and of a lesson being restarted straight after it hits its limit
(H-RAY-005). Assuming one device may be shared led to the risk of a child's
records being saved under the wrong profile (H-RAY-006). Finally, the
supervisor's advice that adults should set the thresholds for each child raised
the risk of a threshold being set to a value that does not suit the child
(H-RAY-007). The specialist interview also shaped several of the mitigations,
such as letting adults mute audio feedback.

\textbf{4. Other than the risk of physical harm, list at least two other types
of risk in software products. Why are they important to consider?}

Most software carries risks that have nothing to do with physical harm. One is
privacy and security. Almost every application stores some information about
its users, and if that information is exposed, accessed by the wrong person or
mixed up between users, it can harm those users and destroy their trust in the
product. This matters even more when the users are vulnerable. In Squigglr, for
example, the records describe a child's difficulties, so they must only be
visible to the adults responsible for that child. A second is data integrity
and availability. People make decisions based on what software shows them, so
data that is lost, corrupted or shown incorrectly can lead them to the wrong
decision, even when nothing visibly crashes. In our case, an adult choosing a
child's next lesson relies on the attempt records being complete and accurate.
A third is psychological harm. Software that frustrates, confuses or misleads
its users can affect their well-being or lead them to wrong conclusions, for
example by implying something about a person that the software cannot actually
judge. For Squigglr, that means avoiding repeated failure without a break and
making sure progress summaries are never read as a diagnosis. These risks are
important to consider because they are easy to overlook when there is no
obvious physical danger, yet they can cause real harm to users and to the
people who rely on the software.
