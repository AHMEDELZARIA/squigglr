\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

This document is the Hazard Analysis (HA) of \progname{}, a website that
children use on a touchscreen device with a stylus to practise drawing, and
that supporting adults use to choose that practice and read its records. The
HA asks how \progname{} could contribute to harm, and turns the answers into
safety requirements in the Software Requirements Specification (SRS). SRS
items are cited by their identifiers (IDs).

\subsection{Background}

\progname{} has guided drawing lessons whose stages give less help each time,
a creative space that refines a drawing a little and can restore the original
(BR-005), a record of every attempt, and progress summaries for supporting
adults (G.1 to G.6). Supporting adults hold the accounts, choose lessons and
starting stages, and decide whether a child goes back a stage (BR-001, BR-003,
CON-007). A passing attempt moves the child to the next stage of the same
lesson without an adult (FR-LRN-023). \progname{} gives no diagnosis, no
judgement of a child's abilities and no comparison with other children
(BR-006).

These conditions of use shape the hazards:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Children may practise with no adult watching (ASM-002), may not be
  able to read (FACT-007) and may share one device (ASM-008).
  \item Adults decide on a child's practice from the records (ASM-006), so a
  record that misstates what the child did can mislead them (BR-004).
  \item Creative drawings may be refined with a pretrained model that the team
  does not train (SRC-002; LC-004).
  \item The website depends on hosting, sign-in and network services outside
  the team's control (ASM-013).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Definition of Hazard}

This analysis uses the definition of hazard from Nancy Leveson's work that the
course adopts \citep{Smith2026L08}. In the wording of
\citet[p.~17]{LevesonAndThomas2018}, a hazard is ``a system state or set of
conditions that, together with a particular set of worst-case environmental
conditions, will lead to a loss''. A loss ``involves something of value to
stakeholders'' \citep[p.~16]{LevesonAndThomas2018}. For \progname{}, the system
is the set of components inside the boundary of Section~\ref{sec:boundary}, and
the environment is everything outside it: the child, the supporting adult, the
device, the network and the outside services. For example, a learner profile
left active after one child's turn on a shared school tablet is a hazard. When
the next child draws without an adult noticing (the environmental condition),
that child's attempts are saved to the wrong record, and an adult later reads a
history that mixes two children (a loss of privacy and of correct records).

\progname{} controls no physical equipment, so the course's ``weakened'' notion
of harm applies: besides injury, harm includes security failures, insensitivity
to users, frustrating software and poor data quality \citep{Smith2026L08}.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} turns this into five losses specific to \progname{}.
Safety is also not the same as correctness \citep{Smith2026L08}: a component can
meet its specification and still contribute to a loss in a harmful situation, so
the analysis looks at conditions of use as well as defects.

\subsection{Changes to the Template}

Every template section is kept, in its original order. The changes are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The losses have IDs L1 to L5, and the components have IDs C1 to C8.
  \item The Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA) tables have a Category
  column. Each team member's rows form one table: Table~\ref{tab:fmea-ray}

  Table~\ref{tab:fmea-mou} (H-MOU) and Table~\ref{tab:fmea-muh} (H-MUH).
  Table~\ref{tab:fmea-random} holds the random failures of the network, the

  \item Rows are not ranked by a risk priority number, as the course advises

  \item Each safety requirement is written once, in the SRS, as the teaching
  assistant (TA) advised (SRC-006). Section~\ref{sec:safety-reqs} lists the
  IDs only.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and Purpose of Hazard Analysis}\label{sec:scope}

The purpose of this analysis is to find the ways \progname{}'s components can
fail, or can work as specified yet still contribute to harm in some situation,
and to recommend for each an action that eliminates the hazard, mitigates it, or
documents and tests it \citep{Smith2026L08}. The actions become requirements in
the SRS, so the analysis is part of specifying \progname{}, not a check made
after it is built. The analysis covers \progname{} as the SRS specifies it for
goals G.1 to G.6, used by children and supporting adults wherever children
practise: at home, in classrooms, and in places such as rehabilitation centres
(SRS, Anticipated Workplace Environment). Hazards are identified per component,
so the scope of the analysis is the system boundary of
Section~\ref{sec:boundary}.

The Category column of the FMEA (Section~\ref{sec:fmea}) classes each row as

loss it leads to.

\subsection{Out of Scope}

The items below are outside the scope of this analysis. Losses or items that are
excluded should be documented \citep[p.~17]{LevesonAndThomas2018}, so each item
has a reason and a stance on what happens to its hazards.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Camera-based finger tracing.} A stretch goal (SRC-001). The
  specialists stated that tracing in the air practises gross rather than fine
  motor skills and should come after the touchscreen activities (FACT-006), and
  no core requirement uses a camera. \emph{Stance:} a camera would capture
  images of the child, while a learner profile holds only an identifier and an
  avatar (BR-001). Before camera tracing is added, this
  analysis must gain a camera component and analyse its privacy hazards; nothing
  here covers them.
  \item \textbf{Letter tracing and drawing.} A stretch goal (SRC-001); two
  specialists saw letters as a later stage after shapes (ASM-010; SRC-009.04).
  \emph{Stance:} letters would be new lessons in the same catalogue (LC-001), so
  the hazards of the canvas, lesson and storage components apply to them
  unchanged. Letter-specific hazards, such as an adult reading letter attempts
  as a handwriting assessment (BR-006), must be added when letters are
  specified.
  \item \textbf{Finger input as a supported input.} A stretch goal (CON-003,
  LC-006); stage checks and value ranges are set for stylus input (ASM-003).
  \emph{Stance:} drawing with a finger on a device without a stylus remains
  possible, and is in scope as a condition of the device outside the boundary,
  since such attempts may be judged wrongly (ASM-003). Only finger input as a
  supported feature, with its own ranges, is out of scope; adding it requires
  the canvas and lesson rows to be reviewed.
  \item \textbf{Offline use.} Out of the initial scope (ASM-005). \emph{Stance:}
  a slow or lost connection is in scope as a condition of the network outside
  the boundary. Only an offline mode, in which \progname{} works without a
  connection, is out of scope.
  \item \textbf{Internal operation of outside equipment and services.} The
  device, browser, network, hosting service, authentication service and
  refinement model are not analysed internally, because the team neither builds
  nor controls them. Physical harm from the device itself, such as a dropped
  tablet, stays with the adult nearby (ASM-002). \emph{Stance:} their failures
  are in scope at the boundary, as causes of failure modes of the components
  that use them (Section~\ref{sec:boundary}).
  \item \textbf{The project's own activities.} Requirements gathering and
  evaluation involve no children (CON-005, CON-006). These are constraints on
  the project, not behaviour of \progname{}. \emph{Stance:} any future study
  with children needs ethics approval first (FACT-008), which this analysis does
  not replace.
\end{itemize}
Diagnosis and treatment are never features of \progname{} (BR-006), so no hazard
of a diagnostic function is analysed. The use of practice records as diagnostic
evidence, however, is in scope, because the records exist and adults may show
them to others (loss L5; H-RAY-003).

\section{Critical Assumptions}\label{sec:assumptions}

The assumptions below are kept few and are written so that they do not remove
hazards. Squigglr does not assume that an adult watches the child during
practice, that the network is always available, or that the device meets
Squigglr's requirements; those conditions are treated as failure modes in the
Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA). Sources are cited by their identifiers
in the Software Requirements Specification (SRS), Appendix A, and interview
turns by speaker and timestamp, for example (SRC-005, P1, 34:13). Each
assumption lists the hazards it affects.

\begin{description}
\item[CA-001] An adult sets up Squigglr for a child, chooses and starts the
lesson and its starting stage, and is the only person meant to accept a
suggestion to go back a stage. Within a lesson the child does not navigate, exit
or restart; Squigglr moves between stages. An adult may not be present or
watching while the child practises.\\
\emph{Rationale:} children should practise without constant adult help, while
adults handle setup (SRC-005, P1, 34:13), and adults decide progression
(SRC-005, P1, 28:37; CON-007).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-001, H-RAY-004, H-RAY-005.

\item[CA-002] The device, browser, touchscreen or stylus in use may not be one
of the supported combinations (\texttt{SUPPORTED\_DEVICES}, TBD), and Squigglr
may be opened on a device without a stylus, where the child can draw with a
finger but not as intended (ASM-003).\\
\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr is a website opened from a web address with no
installation (CON-001, CON-002), so the team does not control the device. The
supported combinations are not yet defined (CON-003), and the team did not
promise that Squigglr works on every device (SRC-005, Moustafa, 6:03).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-002, H-MUH-014.

\item[CA-003] The network connection may be slow or lost at any time, including
during an attempt.\\
\emph{Rationale:} attempts are saved to an account and reviewed later from
another device, and offline access was not promised (SRC-005, Moustafa, 6:03).
Home and school networks are outside the team's control.\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-006, H-SHA-003, H-MUH-009 to H-MUH-011.

\item[CA-004] One device may be used by several children, at school or at
home.\\
\emph{Rationale:} school devices may be shared by several children (SRC-004),
and Squigglr may be used at home, at school or in other settings such as
rehabilitation centres (SRC-005, P2, 21:25).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-006.

\item[CA-005] The adults who read practice records are not assumed to have
training in visual-motor development, and they may show the records to other
people, such as teachers or therapists.\\
\emph{Rationale:} parents and caregivers are not assumed to be specialists (SRS,
Hands-On Users), and records exist to support conversations about what a child
needs (SRC-005, Moustafa, 6:03). Squigglr makes no diagnosis or treatment claims
(SRC-001).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-003.

\item[CA-006] The supporting adult chooses the adult-set values, such as
\texttt{STAGE\_PASS}, \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}, \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX}, for each child within a range the team sets (SRC-008). The ranges are not found
from child testing, and the value an adult picks, or the default, may not suit
their child.\\
\emph{Rationale:} no information is gathered from children and no study with
children is run (CON-005, CON-006), so the ranges cannot be tuned on children
(SRS, Goals of the Project). Children reach these skills at different points
(SRC-005, P1, 20:27).\\
\emph{Hazards affected:} H-RAY-004, H-RAY-005, H-RAY-007.
\end{description}

\section{Failure Mode and Effect Analysis}\label{sec:fmea}

This section applies Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (FMEA) to the components
of Table~\ref{tab:components}. Each team member's rows are in one table
(Tables~\ref{tab:fmea-ray} to~\ref{tab:fmea-muh}), random failures are in
Table~\ref{tab:fmea-random}, and Table~\ref{tab:fmea-coverage} checks every
component against every category of harm.

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:fmea-method}

FMEA starts from the functions of each component and asks how each can fail
\citep{Smith2026L08}. The steps were:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Take each component's functions from Table~\ref{tab:components} and
  apply four guide words: not performed, performed wrongly or in part,
  performed at the wrong time, and performed when it should not be.
  \item Check every component against the five categories of harm (Physical,
  Psychological/well-being, Privacy/security, Data integrity and
  Availability), so that a component that works as specified but still harms a
  child is recorded. For creative refinement, this check also covered the
  risks of artificial intelligence (AI) \citep{SlatteryEtAl2024}.
  \item Each member writes the rows for the parts of the SRS they own, so each
  hazard ID has its writer's prefix.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A row needs a real trigger.} Every cause is one of two kinds. A
\emph{real-world trigger} comes from outside \progname{}: the child, the
adult, the device, the network or an outside service. A \emph{defect} is only
\progname{} failing to meet a stated requirement. A row is kept only if at
least one of its causes is a real-world trigger. A failure that needs a defect
and nothing else is not a hazard here: the Verification and Validation (VnV)
Plan tests that requirement.

\paragraph{Columns.} The Comp.\ column gives the ID of the row's main
component (C1 to C8). Each recommended action begins with \emph{Eliminate},
\emph{Mitigate} or \emph{Document/Test}. The last column names the SRS
requirements that carry out the action. An ID in bold is a requirement this
analysis created, because the hazard showed that no requirement covered it. An
ID in plain type is a requirement the SRS already had. In a row with several
causes, the labels (a), (b) and so on link each cause to its action and its
requirements.

\paragraph{No ranking.} Rows are not ranked, and no row is left out because
its failure seems unlikely. The course advises against risk priority numbers
\citep{Smith2026L08}, and \progname{} has no usage data from children to base
them on (CON-005, CON-006).

\paragraph{Duplicates.} When two rows share a failure mode and a cause, one
row keeps the shared cause, and the other refers to it and keeps its own ID.

\section{Appendix --- Reflection}

\begin{enumerate}

\item What went well while writing this deliverable?

\textbf{Rayan:} Identifying the hazards was easier than expected because we
already had good sources to work from. The transcript of our interview with
three domain specialists on October 1, 2026 gave us concrete statements, with
timestamps, about how the intended children use apps and what could go wrong for
them. For example, the specialists said adults should decide progression rather
than the app, that audio feedback should be possible to mute, and that Squigglr
may be used at home, at school or in other settings. Each of these pointed
directly to a failure mode we could analyse, so we were not guessing at hazards
from scratch. Our meeting with our supervisor, Dr.~Anand, on October 14, 2026
helped in the same way: once Dr.~Anand explained that thresholds should be set
by the adult for each child within a range, the hazards around wrong or extreme
threshold values became clear. Because the SRS already recorded these sources,
business rules and assumptions, we could trace each hazard and critical
assumption back to a specific statement, which also made the mitigations easier
to justify.

\textbf{Shamil:} Identifying mitigations and elimination strategies for the
hazards went well for me because I have previously taken classes on requirements
and testing. I was able to select proper testing methods like integration
testing or identify which of my original testing ideas were not fit for the
project. For instances where the AI refinement provider returned inaccurate or
inappropriate content, I considered fuzz testing to assess the durability and
coverage of the API guardrails. After realizing that this would require human
confirmation regardless, I decided to just have a human tester recreate the
problematic outputs after modifying the guardrails. Similarly, I recognized that
grey-box testing was suited for the case in which confidential user data is
mistakenly shared with a non-essential third-party service (H-SHA-002). Testers
can run different scenarios and observe the public-facing API to see if the
target content is being shared or not. The requirements course also went over
testing in relations to requirements, so I was able to connect the safety
requirements to specific hazards without issue.

\textbf{Ahmed:} What went well for me was having the learning activity
requirements written in enough detail that I could actually think through how
they might fail. I had been working on the guided lesson flow in the SRS, so I
already knew the steps from drawing a stroke to classifying an attempt, showing
feedback and deciding whether the learner advances. Looking at each of those
steps made it much easier to come up with specific hazards instead of just
listing general problems that any website could have.

It also helped that we were working on the SRS and Hazard Analysis at the same
time. When I identified a failure mode, I could check whether an existing
requirement already addressed it and point to that requirement directly. For
duplicate stylus events and incorrect coordinate mapping, I could see that we
needed two more explicit safeguards in the SRS. I think that connection between
the two documents was the strongest part of my work, because the hazards led to
concrete requirements that we can eventually test.

\textbf{Moustafa:} What went well was how much this deliverable changed the way
I think about what we're building. When I think of developing a drawing app for
kids, the last thing that comes to mind is hazards and safety. But going through
my rows made me realize how many gaps we could have had if we hadn't thought
about this. For example, I never would have guessed a drawing app could cause
physical harm, but rapid flashing can trigger seizures in people with
photosensitive epilepsy, which is why WCAG limits flashing to three times a
second. That became H-MOU-001 and SAF-MOU-003. I think thinking in terms of
hazards early on is going to be really useful for my career in general, not just
for this project.

\textbf{Huzaifah:} What went well was writing my SRS requirements and my hazards
at the same time. I wrote both the creative drawing, attempt record and progress
summary requirements and the hazards for those parts, so every H-MUH hazard
could point to a requirement I had written. When a hazard showed a gap, I added
the missing requirement straight away: FR-CRT-029 shows whether every change to
a creative drawing has been saved, and FR-CRT-030 lets refinement be turned off.
Working on both together kept the two documents consistent with each other.

\item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how did
you resolve them?

\textbf{Rayan:} The main pain point was that my hazards kept changing as other
parts of the documents changed. I wrote my first set of hazards and critical
assumptions based on the SRS as it stood at the time, where the team would set
fixed values for thresholds such as \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and

reviewed the hazard analysis again, I found that this changed the hazards: the
risk was no longer only a wrong team-chosen value, but also an adult choosing a
value at the edge of the range, so I added a new hazard and safety requirement
for it. The same thing happened when I added the improvement rule, which can
extend a lesson while a child is improving, and when we confirmed that the child
does not navigate inside a lesson. Each time, I had to go back and update the
affected hazards, critical assumptions and mitigations so they matched the SRS.
It was time-consuming, but it showed me how closely the hazard analysis depends
on the requirements, and that it has to be reviewed again whenever the SRS
changes.

\textbf{Shamil:} My main pain point was in producing FMEA items that would
translate to non-overlapping requirements. I accomplished this well by making
unauthorized data access (H-SHA-001) and data leakage to third parties
(H-SHA-002) distinct. These two could have been unauthorized data access in
general, however this would not cover cases in which our own service leaks data
unknowingly. Splitting these cases into two rows also gave room to discuss
account access in general, which would not apply to third-party data leakage.

\textbf{Ahmed:} The hardest part was deciding where one hazard ended and another
began. A mistake in the drawing input can lead to a wrong classification, which
can then lead to incorrect feedback or an inappropriate stage change. At first
it was tempting to describe all of those consequences in every row. I had to
keep going back and asking what the actual failure mode was, what caused it, and
which requirement would prevent or reduce it.

There was also some overlap with the hazards being written by other team
members. In particular, my initial feedback hazard was too close to the work on
confusing or discouraging feedback. After reviewing that feedback, I narrowed my
row to a technical problem: the system choosing the wrong pre-approved message
for the classification it produced. This left the wording, tone and sensory
aspects to the other hazard owner. Another challenge was keeping the references
aligned as the SRS changed. Whenever we clarified a lesson rule or added a
safety requirement, I had to check the FMEA again rather than assuming the old
wording still worked. It took extra revisions, but the result was more precise
and easier to review.

\textbf{Moustafa:} The main pain point was that some of my hazards depended on
requirements nobody had written yet. For example, the hazards about refinement
changing a drawing too much and progress views misleading adults belong with
Muhammad's creative-drawing and progress-summary requirements, which weren't
written when I was doing my rows. I couldn't just leave the mitigation column
empty, so where a goal or business rule already covered the hazard, I traced to
that, and where nothing did, I wrote my own safety requirements, SAF-MOU-001 and
SAF-MOU-002. It also meant my rows came in late, because I needed my own
usability sections done first so the hazards had something to point to.

\textbf{Huzaifah:} My main pain point was that some of my hazards overlapped
with my teammates' hazards. For example, H-MUH-004, where work is saved under
the wrong attempt or child, covers some of the same ground as Rayan's H-RAY-006,
where the wrong learner is active on a shared device. Writing the same cause
twice would have given two sets of mitigations to keep in sync. I resolved this
by linking to the existing hazard instead of copying it, so each cause is
analysed in only one place.

\item Which of your listed risks had your team thought of before this
deliverable, and which did you think of while doing this deliverable? For the
latter ones (ones you thought of while doing the Hazard Analysis), how did they
come about?

\textbf{Rayan:} Before this deliverable, the team had already thought of two of
my risks. Because Squigglr runs in a browser, we expected that attempts could be
lost or not saved on an unreliable browser or device (H-RAY-002). We also knew
that adults might read a child's records as a diagnosis (H-RAY-003), which is
why the problem statement already avoids any diagnostic claims.

The other five risks I only thought of while doing the hazard analysis. They
came about because this was the first time I had to work out the requirements
for the application in detail, while writing the SRS alongside this document.
Most of them came from the critical assumptions, and several of those
assumptions came from our interview with the specialists on October 1, 2026. The
specialists said children can use the app through images and icons while adults
handle setup, which led to the assumption that an adult sets up each session.
Asking what happens if no adult is actually watching led to the risk of a child
reaching adult-only actions (H-RAY-001), and, together with defining what
happens after repeated failed attempts, to the risks of a child becoming
distressed (H-RAY-004) and of a lesson being started again straight after it ends
(H-RAY-005). The specialists also said Squigglr may be used at home, at school
or in other settings, which led to the assumption that a device may be shared
and to the risk of a child's records being saved under the wrong profile
(H-RAY-006). The last risk came from our meeting on October 14, 2026, where
Dr.~Anand advised that adults should set the thresholds for each child; this
raised the risk of a threshold being set to a value that does not suit the child
(H-RAY-007).

Looking back, most of my risks only became visible once the requirements were
concrete enough to question. The risks we knew about beforehand were general
ones that apply to almost any browser app, while the ones I found during this
deliverable were specific to how children and adults will actually use Squigglr.
This showed me that the hazard analysis cannot be done well in isolation; it
depends on knowing the requirements and on hearing from people who understand
the users.

\textbf{Shamil:} We thought of hazards related to unauthorized account access
(H-SHA-001) before the hazard analysis simply because it was obvious and a basic
need to be addressed. Additionally, we had an idea of the issues that would come
with using a third-party AI provider, including PII leakage to the provider
(H-SHA-002) which are also obvious. The hazard of false save confirmation
(H-SHA-003) came to use later as we consulted more experts and realized the
value of user trust, especially regarding transparency for parents of child
users. We did not want to mislead parents or have them find out too late that
their childs progress was not saved; after brainstorming, we chose to tackle
this by making the saving confirmation more robust.

\textbf{Ahmed:} Before we started this deliverable, I had already thought about
the possibility of drawing input being incomplete and of an incorrect attempt
being allowed to pass. Those were concerns that came up naturally while defining
how a guided lesson should evaluate strokes and move a learner to the next
stage. They became H-AHM-001 and H-AHM-004 once I described their causes,
effects and mitigations more systematically.

The FMEA made me consider several less obvious failures. For H-AHM-002, I
started thinking about what happens when input events are reported more than
once. Two overlapping strokes might be perfectly intentional, but one event
being duplicated by the software is a different problem, and simply deleting
repeated-looking lines would not solve it. H-AHM-003 came from looking at how
the canvas changes size: even if the stylus reports valid points, a scaling or
coordinate-mapping mistake could shift the drawing before it is scored. Those

I also looked beyond the drawing itself. H-AHM-005 came from asking whether a
correct classification could still produce the wrong feedback if the message

there could suggest a stage change at the wrong time or apply one without
permission. Finally, H-AHM-007 came from considering what happens when a
learner's saved settings are changed or profiles are switched. That exercise
showed me that a lot of meaningful risks are not obvious until you follow the
data and decisions across the entire workflow.

\textbf{Moustafa:} Most of my risks were things we had already thought about

H-MOU-005 and H-MOU-006. The other two came up while doing this deliverable.
Sensory overload (H-MOU-001) came from the questionnaire, where one specialist
warned about excessive visual or auditory stimuli, and the flashing part came

(H-MOU-003) came from the same work: once I let adults mute audio and said
feedback can't rely on colour alone, I started asking what happens to a child
who gets sound-only or colour-only feedback, or feedback they need to read.

\textbf{Huzaifah:} Three of my risks were ones the team had thought of before
this deliverable. We had already planned that refinement would only tidy the
child's own drawing and that the original could always be restored, which is
where a restore that is not exact (H-MUH-001) and a refinement that changes the

The other five came up while doing the hazard analysis: records that no longer

\item Other than the risk of physical harm (some projects may not have any
appreciable risks of this form), list at least 2 other types of risk in software
products. Why are they important to consider?

\textbf{Rayan:} Most software carries risks that have nothing to do with
physical harm. One is privacy and security. Almost every application stores some
information about its users, and if that information is exposed, accessed by the
wrong person or mixed up between users, it can harm those users and destroy
their trust in the product. This matters even more when the users are
vulnerable. In Squigglr, for example, the records describe a child's
difficulties, so they must only be visible to the adults responsible for that
child. A second is data integrity and availability. People make decisions based
on what software shows them, so data that is lost, corrupted or shown
incorrectly can lead them to the wrong decision, even when nothing visibly
crashes. In our case, an adult choosing a child's next lesson relies on the
attempt records being complete and accurate. A third is psychological harm.
Software that frustrates, confuses or misleads its users can affect their
well-being or lead them to wrong conclusions, for example by implying something
about a person that the software cannot actually judge. For Squigglr, that means
avoiding repeated failure without a break and making sure progress summaries are
never read as a diagnosis. These risks are important to consider because they
are easy to overlook when there is no obvious physical danger, yet they can
cause real harm to users and to the people who rely on the software.

\textbf{Shamil:} Loss of privacy and trust in the application are two types of
risks other than physical harm that are important to address. Having one's PII
leaked can cause emotional distress and fear for safety. It is also irreversible
once a malicious actor has access or if the information becomes public.
Furthermore, it leads to the second harm which is loss of trust. This harms the
products reputation and prevents potential customers from considering an
otherwise helpful product.

\textbf{Ahmed:} One important type is data integrity risk. Software can appear
to work while quietly saving or interpreting the wrong information. That matters
for Squigglr because a drawing could be captured incorrectly, marked as passing
when it should not, or saved under the wrong result. Adults rely on those
records to understand what happened during practice, so an inaccurate record is
not just a technical bug; it can influence their next decision.

Another type is privacy and security risk. A system may expose one person's
information to someone who should not have access to it, or mix records between
different users. Squigglr may be used on shared devices, and its records concern
children, so we need to think about who can see each learner's history and how
the active learner is identified. Psychological or well-being risks also matter.
Incorrect feedback, repeated failures or unexpected progression can confuse or
frustrate a child even if there is no physical injury. These examples reminded
me that software safety is about more than preventing a crash or a hardware
failure. It also means making sure people can trust what the system tells them
and that it behaves in a way appropriate for its users.


\textbf{Moustafa:} Two other types of risk are accessibility risk and legal or
compliance risk. Accessibility risk is when some people simply can't use the
software, and it's easy to miss because teams usually test on people like
themselves. For Squigglr, a child who can't read yet, is colour-blind or has
trouble with fine motor control could fail to use the app without anyone
noticing, which is the opposite of what we're building it for. That's what most
of my usability and accessibility requirements are there to prevent. Legal and
compliance risk is when software breaks a law or regulation, which can get it
pulled or fined even if it works fine. One of our specialists pointed out that
WCAG is adopted as law in many countries, and Squigglr keeps records about
children, so we have to think about both accessibility and privacy rules before
we can put it in front of real families or schools.

\textbf{Huzaifah:} Two other types of risk are data integrity and over-reliance
on AI. Data integrity risk is when software saves or shows information that is
wrong or incomplete, even though nothing visibly breaks. It matters because
people make decisions based on what software shows them. In Squigglr, an adult
could choose a child's next lesson from a record or summary that does not match
what happened (H-MUH-007, H-MUH-008). Over-reliance on AI is when people trust
an AI's output without checking it, even when it is wrong or changes too much.
It matters because AI output can look convincing while being incorrect, which is
why refinement in Squigglr only makes small changes, stays editable and can
always be undone.

\end{enumerate}
