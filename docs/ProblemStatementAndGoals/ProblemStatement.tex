\section{Problem Statement}

\subsection{Problem}

Visual-motor integration is the ability to coordinate visual information with
movement. Children rely on it when drawing, copying shapes, and learning to
write. Research links these skills to handwriting legibility and identifies
motor or handwriting difficulties in some children with
attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or autism [1--3]. The need
extends from kindergarten and primary school learners to older children who
require continued support.

Children need opportunities to practise at an appropriate level, understand
their difficulties, and develop greater independence. Parents and educators
need to distinguish what a child can do independently from what they accomplish
with assistance. A finished drawing alone does not provide that distinction.
Research on structured foundational activities supports the value of practice
for visual-motor development [4].

There is an opportunity to build a system that supports drawing practice suited
to individual abilities, provides understandable feedback, and helps children
and supporting adults track independent progress. The intended outcome is
improved drawing skills and greater independence, with opportunities for
creative expression. Supporting adults need access to both individual attempt
records and understandable summaries of change over time. Clinical diagnosis
and treatment are outside the project scope.

\subsection{Inputs and Outputs}

\subsubsection{Inputs}

\begin{itemize}
\item Selected drawing activities and support preferences.
\item Children's drawing attempts during lessons and creative activities.
\item Editing actions, such as moving, resizing, recolouring, or erasing
  drawing elements.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Outputs}

\begin{itemize}
\item Drawing targets, demonstrations, and guidance appropriate to the activity
  and support level.
\item Simple activity feedback and completion results based on predefined
  observations.
\item Editable creative artwork, including supported automatic refinements and
  recoverable original drawings.
\item Attempt records showing encountered difficulties, changes within an
  attempt, guidance used, and the final outcome.
\item Progress summaries showing changes across attempts and recurring
  difficulties, with access to the supporting records.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stakeholders}

\begin{itemize}
\item Kindergarten and primary school children.
\item Children of different ages who need additional support, including
  children with autism, ADHD, or other developmental or learning differences.
\item Parents, guardians, and caregivers.
\item Educators and learning-support staff.
\item School administrators.
\item Domain advisers in inclusive education and accessible educational
  software.
\item Dr.\ Christopher Anand, project supervisor.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Environment}

Users will need access to a supported web browser on a compatible device with a
display and a supported drawing input. Using the application will not require
installing a separate application.

\section{Goals}

\subsection{G1. Provide structured practice in lines, shapes, and simple drawings}
Provide structured opportunities to practice lines, shapes, and simple drawings that 
involve visual-motor coordination. This goal is achieved when users can complete 
practice activities covering the agreed foundational skills, receive feedback, and 
repeat an activity. The team will verify this by completing 
each activity in the lesson catalogue.


\subsection{G2. Enable progression from tracing to drawing without guides}
Enable learners to progress from tracing to drawing with reduced guidance and eventually
without guides. This goal is achieved when the application supports this progression
and restores assistance following repeated difficulty. The team will verify both
directions using prepared sequences of successful and unsuccessful drawing attempts.

\subsection{G3. Adapt drawing activities to individual guidance and difficulty needs}
Allow learners with different starting abilities and support needs, including
children with autism or ADHD, to access suitable practice. This goal is achieved when 
the same activity can be completed with different guidance and difficulty settings.
The team will verify that changing these settings produces the intended differences
in the activity.


\subsection{G4. Support free drawing with automatic refinement}
Allow children to draw freely beyond structured lessons, with automatic assistance
for supported shapes and simple objects. This goal is achieved when users can create 
drawings of their choosing, receive automatic refinement, and continue editing the result
while retaining the ability to restore their unmodified drawing. The team will verify
this by creating and refining supported drawings in the creative space, then modifying
and undoing the corrections.


\subsection{G5. Record activity attempts and encountered difficulties}
Help parents and educators understand a child’s progress by recording
each activity attempt, the guidance provided, difficulties encountered,
and the final outcome. Difficulties will be captured through predefined observations
appropriate to the activity, such as missed tracing points or incomplete lines.
This goal is achieved when attempts can be reviewed and compared without
losing earlier difficulties or overlooking improvements made within an attempt.
The team will verify this using prepared attempts with known difficulties and
outcomes, confirming that the stored records and progress summaries accurately reflect them.


\subsection{G6. Help parents and guardians understand their child's progress and practice needs}
Allow parents and guardians to review their child’s activity history,
recognise improvements, and identify recurring difficulties that may warrant
further practice or discussion with an educator. This goal is achieved when
an authorised adult can access a summary of completed activities, changes
in performance, and assistance used, and inspect the attempts supporting that
summary. The team will verify this using prepared histories showing
improvement, repeated difficulties, and changing support levels, checking 
that each summary accurately represents the underlying records.

\section{Stretch Goals}

\textbf{Camera-based finger tracing.} Display shapes, tracing guides, and
start and end points over a live camera view. Children would see themselves
in the background and trace the guides by moving their finger, offering
another fun way to practise visually guided movement.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Letter tracing and drawing.} Extend the activities to letters,
allowing children to progress from tracing letter outlines to forming
letters without guides.

\textbf{Fall --- Literature Review Report}

We will review contemporary research on drawing practice and its relation to 
visual-motor development. We will also look the learning and support needs of 
children, including those with autism or ADHD. The literature review report 
will compare findings to identify limitations and explain how the evidence 
informs the application's activities, guidance, and progress tracking.

\textbf{Winter --- Usability Report}

We will evaluate the usability of our drawing activities and parent-facing 
progress summaries. The report will document evaluation methods and criteria 
as well as observed difficulties and recommended improvements. Such methods 
will include team walkthroughs with adult reviewers, not depending on child 
participation.

\begin{enumerate}

\item What went well while writing this deliverable?

\textbf{Rayan:} Through my parents, I connected with a specialist who holds a
doctorate and develops software for children with autism, as well as the head
of a nonprofit organisation serving people with additional support needs,
including autism and ADHD. These connections gave us a clearer direction for
identifying stakeholders who could inform our requirements. They also helped me
recognise the value of seeking both software-design expertise and experience
supporting our intended users. We now have potential contacts who can help us
question our assumptions and understand needs that we might otherwise overlook.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Ahmed Elzaria:} What went well was developing a clearer
connection between our users' needs and the goals of the application.
I found the distinction between completing a drawing and completing it
independently particularly useful. A finished drawing can look successful
without showing how much guidance the child needed. Recording assistance
and encountered difficulties therefore gives the progress summaries a
clearer purpose than simply displaying completed artwork.

Working on the Development Plan alongside this document also helped me
consider whether our proposed approach supported the stated goals. For
example, automatic refinement should leave children with artwork they
can continue editing, which affects what we need to investigate in the
proof of concept. I learned that dividing the documents between team
members does not remove the need to check their consistency. For future
milestones, I would make this comparison an explicit review step so that
our technical plans continue to address the agreed user needs.

\par\medskip
\textbf{Moustafa:} After the first tutorial in the second week,
Rayan and I discussed two project ideas with our TA, Chris Schankula.
Chris provided valuable insights into what we should consider and how
we could approach the drawing application. He also recommended
Dr.~Anand as a potential supervisor. This discussion helped us evaluate
the idea more clearly and gave us the confidence to pursue it. Having
an early opportunity to discuss the project with someone knowledgeable
helped establish a direction before we committed further effort to the
proposal.

\textbf{Muhammad Huzaifah:} Our team connected easily around the high-level
idea and the problem we wanted to solve. This shared understanding helped us
align on the problem statement and goals, because we had a common purpose to
refer back to when discussing what belonged in the document. I found that
agreeing on the problem first made it easier to discuss goals without getting
drawn too early into implementation details. My main takeaway is that time
spent establishing a shared direction makes later decisions more focused and
helps individual contributions fit together.

\item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how did
you resolve them?

\textbf{Rayan:} When I first presented our idea to Dr.\ Smith, he explained that
we needed to justify the need for the application. I realised that describing
useful features was not enough; we needed evidence supporting the problem we
wanted to address. I reviewed research on visual-motor development, drawing
practice, and difficulties experienced by some children with autism or ADHD.
Comparing these findings helped me connect our proposal to existing work and
explain the rationale for structured drawing practice. It also clarified the
distinction between evidence supporting our approach and evidence that our
particular application will be effective. In future deliverables, I would
establish the evidence and its limitations earlier so that they guide our scope
from the beginning.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Ahmed Elzaria:} A challenge for me was separating an
interesting technical feature from a convincing reason to build it.
Our earlier idea included drawing in the air using a camera, but the
professor's feedback challenged whether that interaction addressed a
meaningful need. This made me reconsider how easily enthusiasm for an
implementation can influence the way we describe the problem.

We responded by focusing the core experience on touchscreen or stylus
drawing and keeping camera-based interaction as a stretch goal. Our
supervisor's recommendation to develop a web application also gave us a
more focused implementation direction. However, choosing a practical
platform does not establish that the application will benefit its users.
I see the research supporting the problem and future stakeholder feedback
as necessary complements to technical feasibility.

Another point that needed clarification was what automatic refinement
would provide. Returning a separate enhanced image would not meet our
intention for children to continue editing their drawings. Clarifying
this distinction helped connect the goal to an observable result.
In future, I would describe what a user must be able to do after each
proposed feature before discussing the technology used to build it.

\par\medskip
\textbf{Moustafa:} We initially struggled to write goals at the
right level. After discussing them with Chris, we concluded that they
were too broad, and he emphasised that they needed to be measurable.
Our first response was to add numerical targets to the existing goals,
but this felt forced and introduced evaluation expectations that depended
on testing with children. We then reconsidered what users should actually
be able to accomplish and wrote new goals with observable completion criteria
that our team could verify. This helped me understand that a measurable
goal does not necessarily require a number to be quantifiable; it needs a clear way to
establish whether the intended outcome has been delivered. I would apply
that distinction earlier when defining goals for future work.

\textbf{Muhammad Huzaifah:} We had minor disagreements about commit and pull
request structures and meeting times. Although we agreed on the project idea,
these discussions showed me that we still had different expectations about
how to work together. We resolved them by giving everyone an opportunity to
explain their preferences and constraints, then choosing an approach that the
majority agreed on. Hearing the reasons behind each view helped us reach a
practical decision. I learned that agreeing on working conventions early is
worth the discussion, because small differences can otherwise create repeated
friction during shared work.

\item How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure they
are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of
appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?

\textbf{Team response:} We narrowed the core scope to structured drawing
practice, progression towards independent drawing, adjustable support, free
drawing with refinement, attempt recording, and parent-facing progress
summaries. We moved camera-based tracing and letter activities to stretch goals
so that they would not distract from completing the core experience. We also
limited activity feedback to predefined observations, such as missed tracing
points, rather than personalised generated advice.

We distinguished user goals from supporting requirements such as account
creation and browser compatibility. We also separated recording individual
attempts from summarising progress, giving each goal a distinct purpose. Our
completion checks now use prepared drawing attempts and activity histories that
the team can evaluate without depending on child participants. These decisions
keep the project manageable while retaining substantial design work in drawing
assessment, adaptive guidance, editable artwork, and accurate progress
reporting.

\end{enumerate}
