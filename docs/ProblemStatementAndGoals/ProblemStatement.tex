\section{Problem Statement}

This section describes the need Squigglr addresses, its inputs and
outputs, the stakeholders involved, and the intended usage environment.

\subsection{Problem}

Visual-motor integration is the ability to coordinate visual information
with movement. Children rely on it when drawing, copying shapes, and
learning to write. Research links these skills to handwriting legibility
and identifies motor or handwriting difficulties in some children with
attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or
autism~\cite{daly2003,kaiser2015,fuentes2009}.
The need for appropriate practice extends from kindergarten and primary
school learners to older children who require continued support.
Research on structured foundational activities supports the value of
practice for visual-motor development~\cite{taverna2020}.

Children need opportunities to practise at a suitable level and gradually
perform activities with less assistance. Parents, caregivers, and
educators need to understand both the difficulties encountered and the
support needed to complete an activity. A finished drawing alone does
not reveal whether a child completed it independently, relied on tracing
guides, or corrected difficulties during the attempt. Comparing final
drawings without this context can obscure changes in independent
performance and make it harder to identify appropriate next steps for
practice.

Squigglr aims to support visual-motor practice through drawing activities
with adjustable guidance and difficulty, while making the practice
process understandable to supporting adults. Individual attempt records
and progress summaries will distinguish independent performance from
assisted completion and show recurring difficulties and changes over
time. Creative drawing will provide opportunities for self-expression
alongside structured practice. The intended benefit is to help children
practise with increasing independence and help supporting adults make
informed decisions about further practice. Clinical diagnosis and
treatment are outside the project scope.

\subsection{Inputs and Outputs}

Squigglr accepts activity selections, drawing attempts, and editing
actions. It produces drawing guidance, activity feedback, editable
artwork, and records that help supporting adults interpret progress.

\subsubsection{Inputs}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Selected drawing activities and guidance or difficulty
    preferences.
    \item Drawing strokes entered through touchscreen or stylus
    interaction during practice and creative activities.
    \item Requests to refine creative artwork and editing actions,
    such as moving, resizing, recolouring, erasing, or undoing changes
    to drawing elements.
    \item Requests from authorised adults to view a child's activity
    history, individual attempts, and progress summaries.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Outputs}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Drawing targets, demonstrations, and guidance appropriate
    to the selected activity and current support level.
    \item Activity feedback and completion results based on predefined
    observations, such as missed tracing points or incomplete lines.
    \item Adjusted guidance or activity difficulty in response to
    performance and selected support preferences.
    \item Editable creative artwork with supported automatic
    refinements and the ability to restore the original drawing.
    \item Attempt records showing the activity, guidance used,
    encountered difficulties, changes during the attempt, and
    final outcome.
    \item Progress summaries showing changes across attempts and
    recurring difficulties, with access to the supporting records.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stakeholders}

Squigglr's stakeholders include the children using its drawing
activities, the adults supporting their practice, and those advising
the project.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Children:}
    Kindergarten and primary school learners are the main intended
    users of the drawing activities. Older children requiring
    continued support may also benefit from appropriately selected
    activities. Their interests include understandable guidance,
    suitable difficulty, and opportunities for independent practice
    and creative expression.

    \item \textbf{Children with additional support needs:}
    Children with autism, ADHD, or other developmental or learning
    differences are included within the intended users. Their
    individual needs inform the flexibility of guidance, difficulty,
    and interaction design; a diagnosis alone does not determine
    the support a child requires.

    \item \textbf{Parents, guardians, and caregivers:}
    These adults support practice outside school and review activity
    histories and progress summaries. They need understandable
    information about assistance used, recurring difficulties, and
    changes in independent performance.

    \item \textbf{Educators and learning-support staff:}
    These stakeholders can inform activity selection and interpret
    practice records alongside their observations of a learner.
    Their interests include suitable learning activities and useful
    information for planning further support.

    \item \textbf{School administrators:}
    If the application is considered for school use, administrators
    may influence its adoption. Their interests include suitability
    for the school setting, device availability, and the resources
    needed to support its use.

    \item \textbf{Domain advisers:}
    Advisers in inclusive education and accessible educational
    software can help the team assess assumptions, identify barriers,
    and evaluate whether proposed activities and feedback are
    appropriate for the intended users.

    \item \textbf{Project supervisor:}
    Dr.~Christopher Anand provides guidance on project scope,
    feasibility, and technical direction.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Environment}

Squigglr is intended for use at home and in educational settings, with
adult support available as appropriate to the learner's needs. Children
will complete drawing activities through a supported web browser on a
compatible touchscreen device, using a finger or a compatible stylus.
Parents, caregivers, and educators will review activity records and
progress summaries through a supported browser on a tablet or computer.

The application will be accessed through the web without requiring a
separate application installation. Internet access is expected for
normal use, including retrieving activities and saving or reviewing
progress. Offline operation is outside the initial scope. Specific
supported browsers and devices will be established through requirements
development and compatibility testing.

\section{Goals}
\renewcommand{\thesubsection}{G.\arabic{subsection}}

The following goals describe the outcomes Squigglr aims to support for
children and supporting adults. Each goal includes an observable
completion check; detailed requirements and test criteria will be
developed in later milestones.

\subsection{Support structured drawing practice}

Provide opportunities to practise visual-motor coordination through
activities involving lines, shapes, and simple drawings. Learners
should be able to follow an activity, receive understandable feedback,
and repeat it for further practice.

This goal is achieved when the agreed lesson catalogue covers these
foundational activities and supports the complete practice cycle.
The team will verify this by completing and repeating each activity,
checking that its instructions, drawing task, and feedback are
consistent with its intended purpose.


\subsection{Support progression towards independent drawing}

Enable learners to practise an activity with progressively less visual
guidance, moving from tracing towards drawing without guides. Learners
should have opportunities to demonstrate what they can complete
independently.

This goal is achieved when the agreed foundational activities support
tracing, reduced guidance, and drawing without guides. The team will
verify this by completing an activity at each support level and
confirming that its records distinguish guided attempts from
independent attempts.

\subsection{Adapt support and difficulty to learner needs}

Adjust guidance and activity difficulty in response to a learner's
performance and selected support preferences. Learners experiencing
repeated difficulty should receive additional support or a simpler
task, while consistent success should allow progression towards less
support or greater difficulty.

This goal is achieved when the application responds appropriately to
different performance histories while respecting the available support
settings. The team will verify this using prepared sequences of
successful, unsuccessful, and mixed attempts, checking that the
resulting adjustments follow the agreed progression criteria.
Specific thresholds will be defined in the requirements.


\subsection{Support editable creative drawing and refinement}

Provide a creative space where children can draw freely and request
automatic refinement of supported shapes and simple objects. The
refined artwork should remain editable within the drawing space so
that children can continue developing their work and restore their
original drawing if desired.

This goal is achieved when users can create a drawing, request a
supported refinement, continue editing the result, and recover the
unmodified drawing. The team will verify this using representative
drawings from the agreed supported set, checking that refinement
preserves editability and that unsuccessful refinement requests leave
the existing drawing unchanged.


\subsection{Preserve meaningful activity records}

Help supporting adults understand how a child completed an activity
by retaining the attempt's guidance level, encountered difficulties,
changes during the attempt, and final outcome. Difficulties will be
described through predefined activity observations, such as missed
tracing points or incomplete lines.

This goal is achieved when an attempt can be reviewed without losing
earlier difficulties or overlooking subsequent corrections. The team
will verify this using prepared attempts with known actions, guidance
levels, and outcomes, checking that the records accurately represent
both the process and the final result.

\subsection{Make progress understandable to supporting adults}

Help parents, guardians, caregivers, and educators recognise changes
in a child's performance and identify recurring difficulties that may
inform further practice. Summaries should distinguish changes in
independent performance from results achieved with additional support.

This goal is achieved when an authorised adult can review activity
history, understand changes in performance and assistance used, and
inspect the attempts supporting each summary. The team will verify
accuracy using prepared histories showing improvement, recurring
difficulties, and changing support levels. Adult reviewers will also
be asked to interpret example summaries and locate supporting attempts
to assess whether the information is understandable.

\section{Stretch Goals}

The following extensions will be considered after the core goals are
met, subject to remaining time and technical feasibility.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Camera-based finger tracing.}

Provide an alternative interaction in which children trace displayed
guides by moving a finger within a live camera view. This extension
would be achieved when the application can track the finger's movement
relative to a guide and provide activity feedback. The team would
verify this using representative tracing attempts under defined camera
and lighting conditions.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Letter tracing and drawing.}

Extend structured practice to an agreed set of letters, supporting
progression from tracing letter outlines to forming letters without
guides. This extension would be achieved when the selected letter
activities support guided and independent attempts, activity feedback,
and attempt recording. The team would verify each activity at the
supported guidance levels.

\section{Custom Documents (CDs)}

The team proposes a Literature Review Report for the fall term and a
Usability Report for the winter term, consistent with the Development
Plan. These choices remain subject to instructor approval.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Fall --- Literature Review Report}

The report will examine research on visual-motor development,
foundational drawing practice, and the learning and support needs of
children, including those with autism or ADHD. It will compare
findings, identify limitations in the evidence, and explain how the
research informs Squigglr's activities, guidance, and progress
tracking. It will distinguish evidence supporting the proposed
approach from evidence of effectiveness that would require evaluation
of Squigglr itself.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Winter --- Usability Report}

The report will evaluate the usability of the drawing activities and
adult-facing progress summaries. It will document evaluation tasks,
methods, findings, limitations, and recommended improvements.
Evaluation will include team walkthroughs and task-based sessions
with adult reviewers, including checks of whether reviewers can
interpret summaries and locate supporting attempt records.

The evaluation will not depend on child participation. Findings from
adult reviewers will inform design improvements, but will not be
presented as direct evidence of usability for children or of
developmental benefits.

\par\medskip
\textbf{Ahmed:} What went well was developing a clearer
connection between our users' needs and the goals of the application.
I found the distinction between completing a drawing and completing it
independently particularly useful. A finished drawing can look successful
without showing how much guidance the child needed. Recording assistance
and encountered difficulties therefore gives the progress summaries a
clearer purpose than simply displaying completed artwork.

Working on the Development Plan alongside this document also helped me
consider whether our proposed approach supported the stated goals. For
example, automatic refinement should leave children with artwork they
can continue editing, which affects what we need to investigate in the
proof of concept. I learned that dividing the documents between team
members does not remove the need to check their consistency. For future
milestones, I would make this comparison an explicit review step so that
our technical plans continue to address the agreed user needs.

\item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how did
you resolve them?

\textbf{Rayan:} When I first presented our idea to Dr.\ Smith, he explained that
we needed to justify the need for the application. I realised that describing
useful features was not enough; we needed evidence supporting the problem we
wanted to address. I reviewed research on visual-motor development, drawing
practice, and difficulties experienced by some children with autism or ADHD.
Comparing these findings helped me connect our proposal to existing work and
explain the rationale for structured drawing practice. It also clarified the
distinction between evidence supporting our approach and evidence that our
particular application will be effective. In future deliverables, I would
establish the evidence and its limitations earlier so that they guide our scope
from the beginning.

\par\medskip
\textbf{Ahmed:} A challenge for me was separating an
interesting technical feature from a convincing reason to build it.
Our earlier idea included drawing in the air using a camera, but the
professor's feedback challenged whether that interaction addressed a
meaningful need. This made me reconsider how easily enthusiasm for an
implementation can influence the way we describe the problem.

We responded by focusing the core experience on touchscreen or stylus
drawing and keeping camera-based interaction as a stretch goal. Our
supervisor's recommendation to develop a web application also gave us a
more focused implementation direction. However, choosing a practical
platform does not establish that the application will benefit its users.
I see the research supporting the problem and future stakeholder feedback
as necessary complements to technical feasibility.

Another point that needed clarification was what automatic refinement
would provide. Returning a separate enhanced image would not meet our
intention for children to continue editing their drawings. Clarifying
this distinction helped connect the goal to an observable result.
In future, I would describe what a user must be able to do after each
proposed feature before discussing the technology used to build it.

\par\medskip
\textbf{Moustafa:} We initially struggled to write goals at the
right level. After discussing them with Chris, we concluded that they
were too broad, and he emphasised that they needed to be measurable.
Our first response was to add numerical targets to the existing goals,
but this felt forced and introduced evaluation expectations that depended
on testing with children. We then reconsidered what users should actually
be able to accomplish and wrote new goals with observable completion criteria
that our team could verify. This helped me understand that a measurable
goal does not necessarily require a number to be quantifiable; it needs a clear way to
establish whether the intended outcome has been delivered. I would apply
that distinction earlier when defining goals for future work.

\par\medskip
\textbf{Muhammad:} We had minor disagreements about commit and pull
request structures and meeting times. Although we agreed on the project idea,
these discussions showed me that we still had different expectations about
how to work together. We resolved them by giving everyone an opportunity to
explain their preferences and constraints, then choosing an approach that the
majority agreed on. Hearing the reasons behind each view helped us reach a
practical decision. I learned that agreeing on working conventions early is
worth the discussion, because small differences can otherwise create repeated
friction during shared work.

\par\medskip
\textbf{Shamil:} Figuring out where to start with Gitlab/Avenue (checklists 
and rubrics) and Github (issues, version control, and templates) was confusing 
at first, but after coordinating online and in person and asking our TA during 
our meeting, our team was on the same page. Also, attendance and participation 
was not the best, but we outlined strict and detailed rules in the team charter 
which our members enforced for productivity.

\item How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure they
are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of
appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?

\textbf{Team response:} We narrowed the core scope to structured drawing
practice, progression towards independent drawing, adjustable support, free
drawing with refinement, attempt recording, and parent-facing progress
summaries. We moved camera-based tracing and letter activities to stretch goals
so that they would not distract from completing the core experience. We also
limited activity feedback to predefined observations, such as missed tracing
points, rather than personalised generated advice.

We distinguished user goals from supporting requirements such as account
creation and browser compatibility. We also separated recording individual
attempts from summarising progress, giving each goal a distinct purpose. Our
completion checks now use prepared drawing attempts and activity histories that
the team can evaluate without depending on child participants. These decisions
keep the project manageable while retaining substantial design work in drawing
assessment, adaptive guidance, editable artwork, and accurate progress
reporting.

\end{enumerate}
