\section{Appendix --- Specialist Questionnaire Responses}
\label{app:questionnaire}

This appendix contains the written responses to a follow-up questionnaire
sent to the three domain specialists who took part in the interview
(Appendix~\ref{app:interview}). Two of the three responded. The form was
anonymous, so the responses are labelled R1 and R2 and cannot be matched
to P1--P3. The responses are reproduced as submitted. The form export cuts
off the end of the first and fourth questions, shown here as ``\ldots''.

\subsection*{Q1}
\emph{Regarding the free drawing space we showed, where a child can draw
anything and AI gently tidies it up; do you think this is helpful for
children, or unnecessary? Its meant to be a fun area where\ldots}

\begin{description}
  \item[R1\phantomsection\label{q:R1-Q1}] I think the free drawing space
  could be very valuable, particularly because it gives children an
  opportunity to explore and practice without the feeling that there is
  one ``correct'' answer. However, I would be cautious about how much the
  AI modifies the child's drawing. The aim should be to support the
  child's own effort rather than replace or correct it. I would prefer the
  AI to provide subtle assistance, suggestions, or optional touch-ups that
  the child can accept or reject. Preserving the child's sense of
  ownership and agency is very important. It may also be useful to allow
  the level of AI assistance to be adjusted depending on the child.
  \item[R2\phantomsection\label{q:R2-Q1}] I feel it is a very nice idea so
  that the children could draw anything and the AI could let's say make it
  better. However, sometimes the unclear lines are important for the
  imagination of the child. For example, a family picture with unorganized
  lines will look better (in the child's perspective) than a very clean
  drawing. Hence, I feel this should be also optional feature and not
  mendatory.
\end{description}

\subsection*{Q2}
\emph{From your experience, what makes practice activities like this
motivating for these children, and what makes them frustrating?}

\begin{description}
  \item[R1\phantomsection\label{q:R1-Q2}] Motivation often comes from
  activities that are achievable, predictable, visually engaging, and
  provide immediate positive feedback. Choice is also important---for
  example, allowing the child to choose what to draw, the theme, or the
  level of difficulty. Small signs of progress and encouragement can be
  motivating without making the activity overly competitive.

  Frustration can arise when the system is too demanding, repeatedly
  highlights mistakes, requires very precise movements, or changes the
  child's work in ways they do not expect. It is particularly important
  that the AI does not unintentionally communicate that the child's drawing
  is ``wrong.'' The system should accommodate different motor abilities and
  drawing styles and should recognize effort and progress, not simply
  accuracy.
  \item[R2\phantomsection\label{q:R2-Q2}] The drawing activity itself is
  likely to be a key source of motivation. However, the experience may
  become frustrating if the activity includes too many demanding or
  overwhelming elements. For example, requiring children to draw using a
  pen or stylus rather than their fingers could make the interaction more
  difficult, whereas finger-based drawing may provide a simpler and more
  intuitive experience.
\end{description}

\subsection*{Q3}
\emph{Would extending Squigglr from shapes, to tracing and forming letters
be useful?}

\begin{description}
  \item[R1\phantomsection\label{q:R1-Q3}] Yes, definitely. I think this
  could be a very useful extension, particularly if it is introduced
  gradually from simple shapes and strokes to more complex letter forms.
  There is a natural progression from basic pre-writing shapes to letter
  formation. However, I would recommend maintaining flexibility rather than
  expecting one very precise tracing pattern. It could also be interesting
  to explore Arabic as well as English letters, particularly in our
  context, as the motor patterns and directionality are different.
  \item[R2\phantomsection\label{q:R2-Q3}] Yes, I believe this could
  represent a natural second stage in the development of the application.
  The initial phase could focus on establishing the effectiveness and
  usability of free drawing and basic shape-tracing activities in
  supporting children's engagement and learning. Once this concept has been
  validated, the application could be extended to include letters and
  numbers.

  A more advanced mode could then introduce sequential tracing activities
  in which children follow individual letters to form a complete word. For
  example, the child could trace the letters ``B-O-Y,'' after which the
  application displays an illustration of a boy. To further enhance
  engagement and reinforce the connection between the written word and its
  meaning, the illustration could also include a simple animation, such as
  the boy waving.
\end{description}

\subsection*{Q4}
\emph{Now that we've talked through everything, what stands out to you
most about Squigglr: what worries you, and what seems most promising?
Anything we didn't ask about that you think we should consider\ldots}

\begin{description}
  \item[R1\phantomsection\label{q:R1-Q4}] I would also strongly recommend
  involving children, parents, educators, occupational therapists, and
  other relevant professionals throughout the iterative design and
  evaluation process. Beyond measuring whether drawings become more
  accurate, it would be valuable to examine engagement, frustration,
  independence, confidence, and whether the skills developed through
  Squigglr transfer to drawing and writing without the system.

  As I also mentioned during the meeting, I see potential for Squigglr to
  serve a much broader population. Its underlying concept could be relevant
  across a range of neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative conditions, as
  well as rehabilitation contexts such as stroke and acquired brain injury,
  and for individuals with learning difficulties or motor challenges.

  For this reason, I would suggest not restricting the development of the
  app too early to one specific condition or case study. Instead, I would
  focus on clearly defining the core capabilities and value that Squigglr
  can offer---such as supporting fine motor skills, visuomotor
  coordination, tracing, drawing, and handwriting---and then explore how
  these capabilities can be personalized and evaluated across different
  user groups. This could significantly broaden both the impact and future
  potential of the application.
  \item[R2\phantomsection\label{q:R2-Q4}] The application itself is very
  promising. However, one concern I have is that the current design may not
  be sufficiently appealing or engaging for children. I would therefore
  recommend dedicating substantial attention to the user interface and
  overall visual design of the application.

  The aim should be to create an interface that is engaging, enjoyable,
  and child-friendly, while at the same time maintaining a simple and
  comfortable interaction experience. In particular, it would be important
  to avoid excessive visual or auditory stimuli that could make the
  application overwhelming. Finding the right balance between an attractive
  and playful design and a calm, minimally stimulating environment could
  significantly improve the children's overall experience and willingness
  to engage with the application.
\end{description}
