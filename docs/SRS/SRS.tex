\section{Stakeholders}

This section identifies who has an interest in Squigglr, who uses it, and
how each group informed the requirements. Sources are cited by their
Requirement Source (SRC) identifiers from Appendix~A. The development team
is not listed as a stakeholder.

Each stakeholder is classed by how directly they use Squigglr.
\emph{Primary} stakeholders use it directly: children, parents, guardians
and caregivers, and educators and learning-support staff.
\emph{Secondary} stakeholders shape it or are affected by it without using
it: the client, school administrators, and the domain specialists.
\emph{Tertiary} stakeholders have an indirect interest: occupational
therapists and educational specialists, and the TA and course
instructors.

\subsection{Client}

The client, a secondary stakeholder, is Dr.~Christopher Anand, the project
supervisor, who acts in a
client-like role (SRC-003). He provides guidance on scope, feasibility, and
technical direction (SRC-001). His binding decisions are recorded in the
Mandated Constraints section (SRC-004).

\subsection{Customer}

Squigglr has no paying customer; it is a capstone project and no sale or
purchase is planned. The adults who would choose to adopt it act as
customers in practice: parents, guardians, and caregivers for home use,
and educators and school administrators for school use (SRC-001).

\subsection{Hands-On Users of the Project}

Squigglr has two groups of hands-on users, both primary stakeholders:
children, who complete drawing activities, and supporting adults, who choose activities and review
records.

\paragraph{Children.} Kindergarten and primary school learners, and older
children who need continued support, including children with autism, ADHD,
or other developmental or learning differences (SRC-001). The intended age
range is not yet defined; it is recorded as an open item, \texttt{AGE\_RANGE}
(TBD). Their characteristics vary widely, and a diagnosis alone does not
determine the support a child needs (SRC-001; SRC-005, Rayan, 2:04).
The characteristics below matter most for the requirements, because they
shape how children read, draw and move through the application without
help:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reading ability:} some children cannot read yet, or may
  not understand written instructions. Squigglr has to communicate in ways
  that do not depend on reading, such as images and icons (SRC-005, P1,
  34:13) and spoken audio that can be muted (SRC-005, P2, 37:39).
  \item \textbf{Motor skills:} fine-motor ability varies; some children find holding
  a stylus difficult, and grip can affect how they draw (SRC-005, P3,
  25:36). Fine-motor control is part of visual-motor practice, so children
  need to practise holding a tool, moving from larger tools to smaller
  ones closer to what schools use (SRC-005, P1, 26:46). Squigglr
  therefore uses a stylus as its main input. Drawing with a finger is the
  easiest starting point (SRC-005, P1, 26:46), and finger input is a
  stretch goal. Some children may also have gross-motor or vision
  challenges (SRC-005, P2, 19:05).
  \item \textbf{Support needs:} children should be able to enter a lesson,
  practise, restart, and move back and forth without constant adult help
  (SRC-005, P1, 34:13). Adults decide when a child moves to a new lesson
  or skips a stage (SRC-005, P1, 28:37).
  \item \textbf{Device experience:} many young children already know how
  to navigate touchscreen applications on tablets (SRC-005, P1, 34:13).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Supporting adults.} Parents, guardians, caregivers, educators,
and learning-support staff. They set up accounts, choose lessons and
starting stages, and review attempt records and progress summaries on a
tablet or computer (SRC-001; SRC-005, P1, 34:13). Their technical
experience is assumed to be that of an everyday web user (assumption). They
need understandable information without scores, diagnostic labels, or
comparisons with other children (SRC-005, Moustafa, 6:03).

\subsection{Personas}

The three personas below are \textbf{fictional}. They do not describe any
real child or adult. Personas are used because
requirements cannot be gathered from children directly, and the supervisor
recommended them for this case (SRC-004). There are at most three, and each is a short life story of a person. Details
that come from a source cite it; all other details were invented by the
team to make the persona concrete. No information was collected from children (SRC-004). Each
persona describes one individual; none represents all children or adults
in its group.

The personas describe people, not the product. They leave out design
choices such as screen layout or feedback wording, so they stay valid if
those choices change. Each persona has a fixed identifier (PER-1 to PER-3)
so that scenarios, use cases, usability tests and later documents can
refer to it. The three personas are linked: Adam is Sarah's son and one of
Janice's learners, so one scenario can follow him between home and school. Invented details should be checked with caregivers and educators when
the opportunity arises; if one changes, the scenarios that depend on it
must be reviewed.

\paragraph{PER-1 (learner): Adam.}
Adam is 15 years old; older children who need continued support are
in scope (SRC-001). He lives with his parents and no
siblings. Adam is autistic and has ADHD. He
loves Spider-Man, colours Spider-Man pictures at home, and tends to fixate
on things he enjoys.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Communication:} he is verbal but speaks in short, simple
  sentences, and most of his responses are memorized. He can say how he
  feels or what he wants, such as being hungry or wanting to play, but
  cannot hold the conversations typical for his age. He understands
  spoken words and instructions well.
  \item \textbf{Reading and writing:} he reads and writes only with a
  parent's support. He can read a few short words he recognizes, such as
  ``yes'' and ``no'', but not longer words or sentences.
  \item \textbf{Learning:} he learns best by watching someone do a task
  first and then copying it, rather than being told what to do without an
  example.
  \item \textbf{Attention:} he loses focus easily. For skill-building
  tasks such as reading or drawing, a caregiver needs to watch over him
  throughout. When he colours, which he enjoys, he can work alone for a
  while, but needs encouragement every now and then to stay focused and
  finish.
  \item \textbf{Frustrations:} he becomes agitated when a task is repeated
  over and over.
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology and tools:} he knows how to use an
  iPad. He has no experience with a stylus, but uses crayons, pens,
  pencils, coloured pencils and markers.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} finish a picture of something he likes and show
  it to his family.
  \item \textbf{Context of use:} at home with his mother nearby, and at
  school in sessions with Janice.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{PER-2 (caregiver): Sarah.}
Sarah is Adam's mother and works full time. She wants Adam
to keep building the drawing and fine-motor skills that support writing. She is not a specialist and learns about Adam's needs from
his teachers and therapists.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology:} an everyday web user who wants
  things to work without setup.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} let Adam practise without being called over
  constantly (SRC-005, P1, 34:13); choose what Adam practises and decide
  when he moves on (SRC-005, P1, 28:37).
  \item \textbf{Review habits:} looks through Adam's practice history on a
  laptop once a week to see which stages he reached and what keeps going
  wrong (SRC-001). Shares what she sees
  with Janice at parent meetings.
  \item \textbf{Concerns:} does not want Adam scored, labelled or compared
  with other children (SRC-005, Moustafa, 6:03); wants Adam's data kept
  private.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{PER-3 (educator): Janice.}
Janice is a teacher at a school for students with special needs who
works with several learners each day, including Adam. She
has a short slot with each learner and little time to set things up
between them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Environment:} the school's devices are managed centrally,
  so she cannot install applications (SRC-003, SRC-004).
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology:} comfortable with the school's web
  tools, but has no administrator rights.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} set a different activity for each learner
  (SRC-005, P2, 29:18); compare a learner's records with what she
  observes in class (SRC-001).
  \item \textbf{Concerns:} switching between learners quickly, and making
  sure one learner's records are never shown to another.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Priorities Assigned to Users}

Table~\ref{tab:userPriorities} assigns priorities. Teachers and students
are the main priority (SRC-003).
Rationale: children are the users the activities serve; educators and
caregivers choose activities and act on the records, so their needs come
next. Administrators affect adoption but not daily use, and the specialists
advise rather than use Squigglr (SRC-006). When needs conflict, a child's
ability to practise independently is weighed against an adult's control of
progression; the adult decides (SRC-005, P1, 28:37).

\subsection{User Participation}

Children do not take part in requirements gathering; their needs are
inferred from adults and from personas (SRC-004). The
requirements were informed by:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the supervisor meeting (SRC-004) and TA meetings (SRC-003,
  SRC-006);
  \item a recorded interview with domain specialists P1--P3 (SRC-005),
  held because children could not be asked directly (SRC-004) and the
  specialists build software for the intended users (SRC-006);
  \item peer review of the Problem Statement and Development Plan
  (SRC-007).
\end{itemize}
Parents, caregivers, and educators have not yet been consulted
(SRC-005, Rayan, 12:31); this is an open item. Planned later participation
includes adult reviewers in usability sessions (SRC-001), possible feedback
from adults with autism or ADHD (SRC-005, P2, 37:39), and the specialists'
offered support for a pilot (SRC-005, P1, 24:03). Any study involving
children requires ethics approval first (SRC-004).

\subsection{Maintenance Users and Service Technicians}

During the project, the development team maintains Squigglr, including
activity content and the hosted website. No maintenance users or service
technicians outside the team have been identified. Who maintains Squigglr
after the project ends is an open item (TBD).
