\section{Purpose of the Project}

This section states the problem Squigglr addresses, who has it, and the
goals the project commits to. The problem and goals come from the Problem
Statement and Goals (PSG) document (SRC-001).

\subsection{User Business}

Visual-motor integration is the ability to coordinate what a person sees
with how they move. Children rely on it when drawing, copying shapes and
learning to write (SRC-001). Kindergarten and primary school learners need
chances to practise these skills at a suitable level and to complete
activities with less help over time. Older children who need continued
support, including some children with autism, attention-deficit/%
hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or other developmental or learning
differences, share this need. A diagnosis alone does not determine the
support a child requires (SRC-001).

The adults around these children have a related problem. Parents,
guardians, caregivers and educators need to understand which difficulties a
child met and how much guidance the child needed. A finished drawing alone
does not show whether the child completed it independently, relied on
tracing guides, or corrected mistakes during the attempt (SRC-001).
Squigglr is a website, used on a touchscreen device with a stylus, that
offers guided drawing practice, creative drawing, and records
that help adults understand that practice. It makes no diagnosis or
treatment claims (SRC-001).

The gap left by current alternatives, as the PSG frames it, is that a
drawing's final result is kept without the process behind it: the guidance
used, the difficulties met and the corrections made. Squigglr's value is
in keeping that process visible to supporting adults while children
practise with gradually less support. The comparison with existing products
appears in the Off-the-Shelf Solutions section of this document.

\subsection{Goals of the Project}

Table~\ref{tab:goals} lists the six project goals, G.1 to G.6, using the
PSG's goal titles (SRC-001). Each goal is
stated as Purpose, Advantage and Measurement: what the goal is, the benefit
it brings and to whom, and how the team will check that it is met. Following
the teaching assistant's (TA's) request for measurable goals (SRC-003) and the supervisor's
direction that no requirements are gathered from children (SRC-004), every
check is done by the team or by adult reviewers using prepared or
adult-made attempts, never with child participants. Where a check needs a
number that has not yet been agreed, it uses a named constant marked TBD.
Each lesson (for example, drawing a square) moves through fixed stages
that give gradually less help: a demonstration, tracing a full outline,
drawing between endpoints only, and drawing with no guide. This sequence
follows the activity the team presented to the domain specialists
(SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}).

G.3 was revised after the PSG was submitted. The submitted PSG said
Squigglr adjusts guidance and difficulty automatically. Following the
domain-specialist interview (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P2}{29:18}),
and as agreed at the TA meeting of Oct 7 (SRC-006), the team changed it
to adult-controlled progression and updated the PSG to match (SRC-001).

\section{Stakeholders}

This section identifies who has an interest in Squigglr, who uses it, and
how each group informed the requirements. Sources are cited by their
Requirement Source (SRC) identifiers from Appendix~A. The development team
is not listed as a stakeholder.

Each stakeholder is classed by how directly they use Squigglr.
\emph{Primary} stakeholders use it directly: children, parents, guardians
and caregivers, and educators and learning-support staff.
\emph{Secondary} stakeholders shape it or are affected by it without using
it: the client, school administrators, and the domain specialists.
\emph{Tertiary} stakeholders have an indirect interest: occupational
therapists and educational specialists, and the TA and course
instructors.

\subsection{Client}

The client, a secondary stakeholder, is Dr.~Christopher Anand, the project
supervisor, who acts in a
client-like role (SRC-003). He provides guidance on scope, feasibility, and
technical direction (SRC-001). His binding decisions are recorded in the
Mandated Constraints section (SRC-004).

\subsection{Customer}

Squigglr has no paying customer; it is a capstone project and no sale or
purchase is planned. The adults who would choose to adopt it act as
customers in practice: parents, guardians, and caregivers for home use,
and educators and school administrators for school use (SRC-001).

\subsection{Hands-On Users of the Project}

Squigglr has two groups of hands-on users, both primary stakeholders:
children, who complete drawing activities, and supporting adults, who choose activities and review
records.

\paragraph{Children.} Kindergarten and primary school learners, and older
children who need continued support, including children with autism, ADHD,
or other developmental or learning differences (SRC-001). The intended age
range is not yet defined; it is recorded as an open item, \texttt{AGE\_RANGE}
(TBD). Their characteristics vary widely, and a diagnosis alone does not
determine the support a child needs (SRC-001; \intref{Rayan}{2:04}).
The characteristics below matter most for the requirements, because they
shape how children read, draw and move through the application without
help:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reading ability:} some children cannot read yet, or may
  not understand written instructions. Squigglr has to communicate in ways
  that do not depend on reading, such as images and icons (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}) and spoken audio that can be muted (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{37:39}).
  \item \textbf{Motor skills:} fine-motor ability varies; some children find holding
  a stylus difficult, and grip can affect how they draw (SRC-005, \intref{P3}{25:36}). Fine-motor control is part of visual-motor practice, so children
  need to practise holding a tool, moving from larger tools to smaller
  ones closer to what schools use (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{26:46}). Squigglr
  therefore uses a stylus as its main input. Drawing with a finger is the
  easiest starting point (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{26:46}), and finger input is a
  stretch goal. Some children may also have gross-motor or vision
  challenges (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{19:05}).
  \item \textbf{Support needs:} children should be able to enter a lesson,
  practise, restart, and move back and forth without constant adult help
  (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}). Adults decide when a child moves to a new lesson
  or skips a stage (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}).
  \item \textbf{Device experience:} many young children already know how
  to navigate touchscreen applications on tablets (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Supporting adults.} Parents, guardians, caregivers, educators,
and learning-support staff. They set up accounts, choose lessons and
starting stages, and review attempt records and progress summaries on a
tablet or computer (SRC-001; \intref{P1}{34:13}). Their technical
experience is assumed to be that of an everyday web user (assumption). They
need understandable information without a single overall score, diagnostic
labels, or
comparisons with other children (SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}).

\subsection{Personas}

The three personas below are \textbf{fictional}. They do not describe any
real child or adult. Personas are used because
requirements cannot be gathered from children directly, and the supervisor
recommended them for this case (SRC-004). There are at most three, and each is a short life story of a person. Details
that come from a source cite it; all other details were invented by the
team to make the persona concrete. No information was collected from children (SRC-004). Each
persona describes one individual; none represents all children or adults
in its group.

The personas describe people, not the product. They leave out design
choices such as screen layout or feedback wording, so they stay valid if
those choices change. Each persona has a fixed identifier (PER-1 to PER-3)
so that scenarios, use cases, usability tests and later documents can
refer to it. The three personas are linked: Adam is Sarah's son and one of
Janice's learners, so one scenario can follow him between home and school. Invented details should be checked with caregivers and educators when
the opportunity arises; if one changes, the scenarios that depend on it
must be reviewed.

\paragraph{PER-1 (learner): Adam.}
Adam is 15 years old; older children who need continued support are
in scope (SRC-001). He lives with his parents and no
siblings. Adam is autistic and has ADHD. He
loves Spider-Man, colours Spider-Man pictures at home, and tends to fixate
on things he enjoys.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Communication:} he is verbal but speaks in short, simple
  sentences, and most of his responses are memorized. He can say how he
  feels or what he wants, such as being hungry or wanting to play, but
  cannot hold the conversations typical for his age. He understands
  spoken words and instructions well.
  \item \textbf{Reading and writing:} he reads and writes only with a
  parent's support. He can read a few short words he recognizes, such as
  ``yes'' and ``no'', but not longer words or sentences.
  \item \textbf{Learning:} he learns best by watching someone do a task
  first and then copying it, rather than being told what to do without an
  example.
  \item \textbf{Attention:} he loses focus easily. For skill-building
  tasks such as reading or drawing, a caregiver needs to watch over him
  throughout. When he colours, which he enjoys, he can work alone for a
  while, but needs encouragement every now and then to stay focused and
  finish.
  \item \textbf{Frustrations:} he becomes agitated when a task is repeated
  over and over.
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology and tools:} he knows how to use an
  iPad. He has no experience with a stylus, but uses crayons, pens,
  pencils, coloured pencils and markers.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} finish a picture of something he likes and show
  it to his family.
  \item \textbf{Context of use:} at home with his mother nearby, and at
  school in sessions with Janice.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{PER-2 (caregiver): Sarah.}
Sarah is Adam's mother and works full time. She wants Adam
to keep building the drawing and fine-motor skills that support writing. She is not a specialist and learns about Adam's needs from
his teachers and therapists.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology:} an everyday web user who wants
  things to work without setup.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} let Adam practise without being called over
  constantly (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}); choose what Adam practises and decide
  when he moves on (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}).
  \item \textbf{Review habits:} looks through Adam's practice history on a
  laptop once a week to see which stages he reached and what keeps going
  wrong (SRC-001). Shares what she sees
  with Janice at parent meetings.
  \item \textbf{Concerns:} does not want Adam reduced to a single score, labelled or compared
  with other children (SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}); wants Adam's data kept
  private.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{PER-3 (educator): Janice.}
Janice is a teacher at a school for students with special needs who
works with several learners each day, including Adam. She
has a short slot with each learner and little time to set things up
between them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Environment:} the school's devices are managed centrally,
  so she cannot install applications (SRC-003, SRC-004).
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology:} comfortable with the school's web
  tools, but has no administrator rights.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} set a different activity for each learner
  (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{29:18}); compare a learner's records with what she
  observes in class (SRC-001).
  \item \textbf{Concerns:} switching between learners quickly, and making
  sure one learner's records are never shown to another.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Rationale:} Shared school devices are often managed by the school,

\emph{Imposed by:} supervisor and TA.\\

\emph{Source:} SRC-003, SRC-004.

\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr targets visual-motor skills, which include fine

\emph{Imposed by:} domain specialists, within the scope agreed with the

\item[CON-007] Squigglr shall not decide when a child moves to another
stage or level; an adult chooses.\\
\emph{Rationale:} Deciding when a child is ready needs an assessment, which

knows the child's level, should make that choice (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}).
Squigglr may suggest going back a stage, but an adult must accept (G.3).\\
\emph{Imposed by:} domain specialists.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} no change of stage or level happens without an
adult choosing or accepting it.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-005.

\section{Relevant Facts And Assumptions}
\label{sec:facts-assumptions}
This section records what is known to be true now, the rules that govern
how Squigglr may be used, and what the team believes but has not verified.
Facts and assumptions are kept apart so that a reader can see which
requirements rest on unconfirmed beliefs. The Hazard Analysis uses the
assumptions as possible causes of hazards: if an assumption turns out to
be false, the requirements that depend on it must be reviewed. Most
requirement identifiers are not written yet, so the entries below cite the
goals (G.1--G.6) and constraints (CON-001 to CON-007) they affect;
requirement IDs will be linked when written.

\subsection{Relevant Facts}
\label{sec:facts}
Each fact below is true at the time of writing and is supported by a
source in Appendix~\ref{app:sources}. Facts from the interview are expert
statements, not observations of children.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[FACT-001] Visual-motor integration is linked to handwriting
legibility in kindergarten children, but the link is a correlation; it
does not show that practising one improves the other (SRC-001;
\intref{Rayan}{2:04}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} Squigglr makes no claim that its practice improves
handwriting or visual-motor skills (BR-006).
\item[FACT-002] The study of structured, graded practice cited in the
Problem Statement had no control group. It supports the idea of graded
practice but does not show that Squigglr will produce the same results
(SRC-001; \intref{Rayan}{2:04}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} effectiveness is not a goal or a fit criterion of this
project.
\item[FACT-003] Motor and handwriting difficulties occur in some autistic
children and some children with ADHD, with severity ranging from mild to
severe; not every child in these groups has them (SRC-001;
\intref{Rayan}{2:04}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} guidance and difficulty are set per child by an adult,
not per diagnosis (G.3).
\item[FACT-004] The specialists stated that deciding automatically when a
child is ready for the next level requires assessment, which is too
complex for the project's time frame, and that an adult who knows the
child should decide (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P2}{29:18}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for CON-007, G.3 and BR-003.
\item[FACT-005] The specialists stated that how a child holds a tool
affects how they draw, that finger input is the easiest start, and that
fine-motor practice needs a held tool, moving from large to small
(SRC-005, \intref{P3}{25:36}; \intref{P1}{26:46}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for stylus input (CON-003) with finger input as a
stretch goal.
\item[FACT-006] The specialists stated that tracing in the air in front
of a camera practises gross rather than fine motor skills, and that it
should come after the touchscreen activities when time is limited
(SRC-005, \intref{P1}{13:42}; \intref{P1}{16:13}; \intref{P2}{16:45}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} camera finger tracing stays a stretch goal.
\item[FACT-007] The specialists stated that young children may not read
yet but navigate games through images and icons, and that a child should
be able to enter a lesson, practise, restart and move back and forth
without constant adult help, while adults handle setup (SRC-005,
\intref{P1}{34:13}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} G.1 and the usability requirements; requirement IDs to
be linked when written.
\item[FACT-008] A study that involves children requires ethics approval;
meeting adult experts for feedback, without involving children, does not
(SRC-004; SRC-005, \intref{P2}{22:56}; \intref{P1}{33:00}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for CON-005 and CON-006.
\item[FACT-009] Installing applications on school-managed devices is
difficult, and a website is easier for Canadian schools to adopt (SRC-003,
SRC-004).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for CON-001 and CON-002.
\item[FACT-010] The specialists recommended starting from the Web Content
Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), which one participant stated are adopted
in law in many countries, and then applying design heuristics for autism
from the literature (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{39:26}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} accessibility and standards requirements; requirement
IDs to be linked when written.
\item[FACT-011] The course fixes the project's deadlines (CON-004,
SRC-002).\\
\emph{Relevance:} scope decisions, such as keeping assessment and camera
tracing out of the core product, follow from the time available.
\item[FACT-012] The only outside evidence so far is one 43-minute
interview with three domain specialists. No parent, educator, school
administrator or occupational therapist has been consulted, and no child
has taken part in any source (SRC-005, \intref{Rayan}{12:31}; SRC-004).\\
\emph{Relevance:} requirements based on what parents and educators need
rest on assumptions (ASM-006) until those groups are consulted.
\item[FACT-013] The interview did not cover several topics that affect
the requirements: how Squigglr compares with existing drawing apps (one
participant noted only that many exist (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{35:36})),
differences between home and school use, whether children or adults value
automatic refinement in the creative space, letter activities, the
relative priority of the features, and how practice records should be
kept, shared or deleted (SRC-005).\\
\emph{Relevance:} the requirements on these topics rest on assumptions
(ASM-010 to ASM-012) and are open to change.
\end{description}

\subsection{Business Rules}
\label{sec:business-rules}
The rules below govern who may do what with Squigglr and what Squigglr
must never do. They hold no matter how Squigglr is designed. Each rule has
an identifier of the form BR-\#\#\#, a rationale and its sources.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[BR-001] Only the adult who holds an account, a parent or an
educator, may create, view and manage the learner profiles under it and
their records. One account can hold several learner profiles. A learner
profile holds only the child's first name and a username that does not
identify the child; no other information about the child is collected.\\
\emph{Rationale:} practice records describe a child's difficulties and
are personal information. Keeping each profile to a first name and a
non-identifying username lets an adult tell children apart, for example on
a shared device, while collecting as little about them as possible. How
records are protected is a privacy requirement.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001, SRC-004; G.5, G.6.
\item[BR-002] Within a lesson, Squigglr moves the child to the next stage
when an attempt meets that stage's pass criteria (\texttt{STAGE\_PASS}).
After \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} (larger than
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}) unsuccessful attempts at one stage, the lesson
ends, and the furthest stage reached and the number of successful attempts
at each stage are saved. If, when \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} is reached, the
average result of the last \texttt{IMPROVE\_WINDOW} attempts is better
than that of the \texttt{IMPROVE\_WINDOW} attempts before them by at least
\texttt{IMPROVE\_MARGIN}, the child is improving and Squigglr allows
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_EXTENSION} more attempts. Total attempts at a stage never
exceed \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX}.\\
\emph{Rationale:} moving forward on a pass checks the drawing against fixed
criteria and does not assess the child (G.2). Ending the lesson stops a
child from repeating a stage they cannot pass without limit, and saving the
progress lets the adult decide what comes next. Extending the limit while
the child improves avoids cutting off practice that is working, and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} keeps the stop. The comparison uses only the
child's own attempts against the stage's fixed criteria, so it does not
assess the child.\\
\emph{Source:} G.2, G.3, G.5.
\item[BR-003] A supporting adult chooses which lesson the child practises
and the starting stage. Squigglr never moves a child
back a stage or changes the lesson on its own. After
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} failed attempts in a row, Squigglr may
suggest going back a stage, unless the child is improving (BR-002); the change happens only if an adult accepts.\\
\emph{Rationale:} deciding readiness needs assessment that is out of
scope (FACT-004), and the adult knows the child's level.\\
\emph{Source:} CON-007; G.3; SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37};
\intref{P2}{29:18}.
\item[BR-004] A guided lesson record keeps the child's actual strokes.
Squigglr never replaces them with a corrected or ideal shape.\\
\emph{Rationale:} adults judge progress from the records; a record that
shows a corrected shape would mislead them.\\
\emph{Source:} G.5; SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}.
\item[BR-005] Refinement of strokes happens only in the creative space.
It is automatic and minimal, the refined drawing stays editable, and the
child's original drawing can be restored, by the child or, if the child
cannot, by the adult.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the work must remain the child's own, and refinement
must never reach the records adults rely on (BR-004). Refinement needs no
action from the child, and an adult can restore the original for a child
who cannot use the control.\\
\emph{Source:} G.4; SRC-001.
\item[BR-006] Squigglr gives no diagnosis, no judgement of a child's
abilities, no comparison with other children, and no claim that its
practice improves any skill.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the team are not occupational therapists and cannot
judge a child's abilities (SRC-008), and the evidence does not support
effectiveness claims (FACT-001, FACT-002).\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001, SRC-008; SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}.
\item[BR-007] Feedback during a guided activity comes from observations
the team defines in advance, such as a missed part of an outline. It is
not personalized advice generated by an AI model.\\
\emph{Rationale:} predefined observations can be reviewed and checked
before release; generated advice to a child cannot.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001; SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}.
\item[BR-008] The supporting adult sets \texttt{STAGE\_PASS},
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}, \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT},
\texttt{IMPROVE\_WINDOW}, \texttt{ATTEMPT\_EXTENSION} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} for each child.
When an adult creates a child profile, Squigglr asks them to choose each
value, and for \texttt{STAGE\_PASS} shows example drawings of what its
minimum and maximum look like. The adult can change the values later. A value the adult does not
choose is set to the middle of its range. Squigglr accepts only values
within the range, only an \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} larger than
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}, and only an \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} at
least \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the adult knows the child, and a value that suits one
child may not suit another (FACT-003). The team sets only the minimum and
maximum, so no single value has to be shown right for every child.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-008; CON-007; Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}.
\item[BR-009] The team sets the minimum and maximum of each adult-set
value, and the fixed values in Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}, using
adult-made drawings, never from testing with children.\\
\emph{Rationale:} no study with children takes place (CON-005, CON-006).\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-004, SRC-008; Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}.
\end{description}

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}
The assumptions below are beliefs the team holds but has not verified.
Each has an identifier of the form ASM-\#\#\#, a rationale, what depends
on it, and the effect if it is false. Assumptions marked as relevant to
safety are inputs to the Hazard Analysis.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[ASM-001] An adult is present when a child starts a guided lesson, to
choose the lesson and the starting stage.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists described adults handling setup and
deciding progression (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P1}{34:13}).
How often this happens at home or in class is unknown.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, BR-003, CON-007.\\
\emph{If false:} a child may start at a level an adult did not choose.
Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-002] A child may continue practising without an adult watching,
but an adult nearby remains responsible for the child's physical safety,
wellbeing and time on the device. Squigglr cannot observe or prevent
physical harm, distress or overuse.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists advised that children should practise
without constant adult help (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}), and the
Problem Statement expects adult support as each learner needs (SRC-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.1, the safety requirements (requirement IDs to be
linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} the safety requirements must cover unsupervised use,
for example limits on session length or sound. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-003] Homes and schools that use Squigglr have a touchscreen
device with a stylus that works in a supported browser.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the stylus is the main input (CON-003), and the
interview example used a tablet with a stylus (SRC-005,
\intref{Rayan}{24:56}). Which devices families and schools own is not
known. The supported device and browser combinations are TBD.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} CON-003, G.1, G.2.\\
\emph{If false:} children without a stylus can still use Squigglr with a
finger, but not as intended: the stage checks and value ranges are set for
stylus input, and tuning them for finger input is a stretch goal.
\item[ASM-004] Supported browsers report stylus strokes in enough detail
(position and order of points) for Squigglr to check an attempt against
\texttt{STAGE\_PASS} and to store the actual strokes.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the stage checks and the records both depend on the
strokes; this has not been tested on any device yet.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.2, G.5, BR-004.\\
\emph{If false:} an attempt may be judged wrongly or stored inaccurately,
which would mislead the adult. The team may then need to deliver Squigglr
as an installed app, if input there is more reliable, which would change
CON-001. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-005] A network connection is available during normal use, for
loading activities and for saving and viewing records. Offline use is out
of the initial scope.\\
\emph{Rationale:} stated in the Problem Statement (SRC-001); school and
home networks have not been checked.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.5, G.6, CON-001.\\
\emph{If false:} attempts may be lost when the connection drops, and an
adult may decide from incomplete records. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-006] The supporting adult can judge from the records when a
child should move on, stay or go back.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists placed this decision with a parent or
educator who knows the child (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}). Squigglr
shows the adult every value it records, such as each attempt's result and
the \texttt{STAGE\_PASS} and \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} in use for that
attempt, and the adult chooses which of them their view shows, so the team
does not have to pick which measures matter.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, G.6, CON-007, BR-003.\\
\emph{If false:} a child may be kept at a level that is too hard or too
easy; the adult views would need more guidance. Relevant to
safety.
\item[ASM-007] The adult who uses the adult views can read the language of
the interface. The interface language is TBD. Children do not need to
read to use the child views.\\
\emph{Rationale:} adult tasks such as reviewing records need text, while
child navigation uses images and icons (FACT-007).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.6 and the usability requirements (requirement IDs
to be linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} adults may misread records or settings.
\item[ASM-008] On a shared device, an adult makes sure the right child
is selected before a session and that another child cannot open the
previous child's records.\\
\emph{Rationale:} school devices may be shared by several children
(SRC-004). One adult account can hold several learner profiles (BR-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} BR-001, G.5.\\
\emph{If false:} attempts may be saved to the wrong child, or a child's
records may be seen by others. Relevant to safety and privacy.
\item[ASM-009] The adults who use Squigglr for a child have the right to
see that child's records, and any consent this needs is obtained outside
Squigglr.\\
\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr cannot verify a parent's or school's authority
over a child; it only knows the adult who holds the account (BR-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} BR-001, the privacy requirements (requirement IDs to
be linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} an unauthorised adult could see a child's records.
Relevant to privacy.
\item[ASM-010] Lines, basic shapes and simple drawings are suitable
practice content for the intended children, without letters.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists did not object to this content, and
nurseries focus on these skills (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{20:27}). Letters
were not discussed (FACT-013) and remain a stretch goal (SRC-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.1, G.2.\\
\emph{If false:} the activity set changes.
\item[ASM-011] Children and adults value automatic refinement in the
creative space, and children understand that the refined drawing is still
theirs.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists did not comment on refinement
(FACT-013); the team has not checked this with any adult.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.4, BR-005.\\
\emph{If false:} refinement may discourage a child, and G.4 would need
to change.
\item[ASM-012] Squigglr serves the same needs at home and in school, so
one set of requirements covers both.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists named homes, kindergartens, schools and
rehabilitation centres as possible settings (SRC-005,
\intref{P2}{21:25}) but did not discuss differences between them
(FACT-013).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, G.6, BR-001.\\
\emph{If false:} separate requirements may be needed, for example for an
educator managing several children.
\item[ASM-013] Schools allow access to the Squigglr website on their
managed devices and networks.\\
\emph{Rationale:} a website is easier to adopt than an installed app
(FACT-009), but no school has confirmed that it would not block the site.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} CON-001, CON-002.\\
\emph{If false:} school use needs approval from administrators.
\item[ASM-014] Strokes drawn by adults, or prepared examples, are close
enough to children's drawing to verify stage checks and refinement.\\
\emph{Rationale:} evaluation uses adults only (CON-005, CON-006).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.2, G.4, BR-009, and the verification plan.\\
\emph{If false:} the range of \texttt{STAGE\_PASS} and the value of
\texttt{REFINE\_TOLERANCE} may be wrong for children, so attempts may be
judged wrongly. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-015] An adult can turn off audio feedback, or stop a session,
if sound or on-screen feedback upsets a child.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists recommended that users can turn audio
off (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{37:39}). Children react to sensory feedback in
different ways, and the team has no information on which feedback upsets
which children.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} the accessibility and safety requirements
(requirement IDs to be linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} feedback must be safe by default rather than relying on
an adult. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-016] A supporting adult can choose adult-set values
(Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}) that suit their child, helped by the example
drawings for \texttt{STAGE\_PASS}, or leave the defaults.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the supervisor advised leaving these values to the adult
(SRC-008); no adult has tried the choice yet.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.2, G.3, BR-008.\\
\emph{If false:} a child may be held at a stage too long or moved on too
soon, which can frustrate them. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-017] A rise in the average result of a child's recent attempts
by at least \texttt{IMPROVE\_MARGIN} reflects real improvement, not chance
variation between drawings.\\
\emph{Rationale:} drawing results vary from one attempt to the next; the
team will check the margin with prepared adult-made sequences, not with
children (CON-005, CON-006).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, BR-002.\\
\emph{If false:} a lesson may be extended for a child who is not
improving, or not extended for one who is. \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX}
still ends the lesson. Relevant to safety.
\end{description}

\section{Appendix --- Requirement Sources}
\label{app:sources}

This appendix is the requirement sources register. Each source has a fixed
identifier (SRC-001 to SRC-007) that is never renumbered. A requirement names
the sources it comes from in its rationale or source field. A statement from
the specialist interview is cited by its source identifier, participant code
and transcript timestamp, for example ``(SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37})''; the statement
identifiers in Section~\ref{sec:src-interview} (for example SRC-005.01) can
be cited instead. The interview is expert-reported evidence only. The three
participants are de-identified domain specialists (P1, P2 and P3). No child
took part in the interview or in any other source listed here.

Table~\ref{tab:src-docs} lists the project documents and meeting sources, and
Table~\ref{tab:src-other} lists the interview and peer-review sources. The
supported requirement identifiers are not listed in these tables yet. They
are added as back-links as each requirement is written, so that every
dependency appears at both ends: the requirement names its source, and the
source lists the requirement. Each source and statement has a \LaTeX{} label
(for example \texttt{src:005.01}), so a requirement can link to it and the
link works in the PDF.

The sources represent users in different ways. Children are the main
beneficiaries, but by the supervisor's direction (SRC-004) no requirement is
gathered from children and no information about them is collected. Their
needs enter the requirements only through adults who know them. The domain
specialists (SRC-005) are the only outside voice so far. Their advice changed
the project: progression is chosen by an adult instead of adjusted
automatically, the stylus is the main input with finger input as a stretch
goal, and camera tracing became a stretch goal. Parents, educators and occupational therapists have not been
consulted yet. The specialists advised doing so (SRC-005.03, SRC-005.06), and
until then the requirements that depend on their views rest on the expert
statements below and are open to change.

\subsection{Key Interview Statements}
\label{sec:src-interview}

This subsection lists the interview statements most relevant to the
requirements. Each is a short paraphrase, not a quote; the exact words are in
Appendix~\ref{app:interview} at the given timestamp.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, style=nextline]
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.01}SRC-005.01 (\intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P2}{29:18}; \intlink{Rayan}{30:20}{30:20--31:02})}]
Deciding automatically when a child is ready for the next level would need
assessment and is too complex for the time frame. An adult who knows the
child (a parent or educator) should choose the level. The team agreed to
leave progression to the supervising adult.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.02}SRC-005.02 (\intref{P2}{29:18})}]
In a past project, a teacher personalized each child's activities through a
separate adult interface that also showed progress.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.03}SRC-005.03 (\intref{P2}{31:42})}]
Which progress measures help an adult should be decided with occupational
therapists or educational specialists, using milestones from the literature
if they exist.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.04}SRC-005.04 (\intref{P3}{24:34}; \intref{P3}{25:36}; \intref{P1}{26:46})}]
Holding a pen affects how children draw and the usability of the app.
Finger input is the easiest start; a larger stylus and then a smaller one
could be later difficulty levels if fine motor practice is a goal.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.05}SRC-005.05 (\intref{P1}{34:13})}]
The app should be child-friendly: a child who cannot read yet should be able
to enter a lesson, practise, restart and move back and forth using images
and icons rather than text, without constant adult help. Adults handle setup.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.06}SRC-005.06 (\intref{P1}{36:26})}]
Positive feedback wording and colours should be designed with feedback from
educators and occupational therapists, built up bit by bit.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.07}SRC-005.07 (\intref{P2}{37:39})}]
Audio feedback is a good idea; offering more than one modality is
encouraged in inclusive design, with the user able to turn the audio off.
Design it carefully with therapists' feedback, and consider adults with ADHD
or autism as reviewers when children cannot take part.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.08}SRC-005.08 (\intref{P2}{39:26})}]
Start with the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), then apply more
specialized design heuristics for autism from the literature.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.09}SRC-005.09 (\intref{P2}{19:05}; \intref{P1}{20:27}; \intref{P2}{20:47})}]
From a universal design view, the skills apply to all children, including
those with motor or sight challenges; designing for disability serves many
groups.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.10}SRC-005.10 (\intref{P1}{13:42}; \intref{P1}{15:12}; \intref{P1}{16:13}; \intref{P2}{16:45})}]
Camera finger tracing in the air practises gross rather than fine motor
skills. It is useful but should come after the core touchscreen tracing
when time is limited.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.11}SRC-005.11 (\intref{P2}{22:56}; \intref{P1}{24:03}; \intref{P1}{33:00})}]
Testing with children needs ethics approval (about three to four months
where P2 works). Meeting experts for feedback without involving children
does not.
\item[{\phantomsection%
  \label{src:005.12}SRC-005.12 (\intlink{Rayan}{41:51}{41:51--42:48})}]
All three participants agreed to the use of the transcript for eliciting
requirements.
\end{description}
