\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\paragraph{Purpose and intended readers.}
This Software Requirements Specification (SRS) defines the externally
observable behaviour, constraints and quality requirements of \progname.
It establishes a shared basis for the development team, the supervisor,
reviewers and future maintainers to understand, design, implement and
verify the product. It specifies \emph{what} Squigglr must provide rather
than prescribing an implementation, except where a stakeholder has imposed
a constraint. The project serves children practising drawing on a
stylus-enabled touchscreen and the supporting adults who configure
activities and review practice records (\srcref{001}, \srcref{004}).
The scope, stakeholders, goals and constraints are established in
Sections~\ref{sec:purpose}--\ref{sec:constraints}; requirements are refined
in Section~\ref{sec:functional} and the subsequent quality sections.

\paragraph{Template and adaptations.}
This document follows the Volere Requirements Specification Template by
Suzanne Robertson and James Robertson~\cite{robertson2012volere}; its
LaTeX skeleton was adapted from the course's Volere template. The team
retains the original headings except where changes clarify the capstone
scope. In particular, Section~\ref{sec:terminology} includes a new
symbolic-constants register; Section~\ref{sec:functional} separates
learning activities, formal specification, creative drawing, attempt
records and progress summaries; and Section~\ref{sec:changes} records
likely and unlikely changes. Section~\ref{sec:traceability} provides
cross-document traceability, while Appendix~\ref{app:sources} records
requirement evidence. A separate Hazard Analysis covers risks, failures
and mitigation measures rather than repeating Volere's risks material.
The source register includes anonymized interview paraphrases with
speaker codes and timestamps, and questionnaire statements identified by
respondent and question code. The de-identified interview transcript is
reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:interview}, providing direct links to the
supporting interview turns (\srcref{005}).

Functional requirements have the prefixes \texttt{FR-LRN},
\texttt{FR-CRT}, \texttt{FR-REC} and \texttt{FR-PRG}, each followed by a

assigned prefix. Hazard Analysis uses owner-prefixed identifiers such as

Identifiers, once assigned, are not renumbered. Each
requirement contains a short title, one testable ``shall'' statement,
rationale where needed, a fit criterion, a priority, a phase, evidence
sources and links to related use cases, requirements or hazards.

\section{Purpose of the Project}
\label{sec:purpose}

This section states the problem Squigglr addresses, who has it, and the
goals the project commits to. The problem and goals come from the Problem
Statement and Goals (PSG) document (SRC-001).

\subsection{User Business}

Visual-motor integration is the ability to coordinate what a person sees
with how they move. Children rely on it when drawing, copying shapes and
learning to write (SRC-001). Kindergarten and primary school learners need
chances to practise these skills at a suitable level and to complete
activities with less help over time. Older children who need continued
support, including some children with autism, attention-deficit/%
hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or other developmental or learning
differences, share this need. A diagnosis alone does not determine the
support a child requires (SRC-001).

The adults around these children have a related problem. Parents,
guardians, caregivers and educators need to understand which difficulties a
child met and how much guidance the child needed. A finished drawing alone
does not show whether the child completed it independently, relied on
tracing guides, or corrected mistakes during the attempt (SRC-001).
Squigglr is a website, used on a touchscreen device with a stylus, that
offers guided drawing practice, creative drawing, and records
that help adults understand that practice. It makes no diagnosis or
treatment claims (SRC-001).

The gap left by current alternatives, as the PSG frames it, is that a
drawing's final result is kept without the process behind it: the guidance
used, the difficulties met and the corrections made. Squigglr's value is
in keeping that process visible to supporting adults while children
practise with gradually less support. The comparison with existing products
appears in the Off-the-Shelf Solutions section of this document.

\subsection{Goals of the Project}

Table~\ref{tab:goals} lists the six project goals, G.1 to G.6, using the
PSG's goal titles (SRC-001). Each goal is
stated as Purpose, Advantage and Measurement: what the goal is, the benefit
it brings and to whom, and how the team will check that it is met. Following
the teaching assistant's (TA's) request for measurable goals (SRC-003) and the supervisor's
direction that no requirements are gathered from children (SRC-004), every
check is done by the team or by adult reviewers using prepared or
adult-made attempts, never with child participants. The acceptance
parameters are named in Section~\ref{sec:constants}. On the supervisor's
advice (SRC-008), parameters that depend on the child are selected by a
supporting adult within specified ranges; tests check that the selected
settings and defaults are respected.
Each lesson (for example, drawing a square) moves through fixed stages
that give gradually less help: a demonstration, tracing a full outline,
drawing between endpoints only, and drawing with no guide. This sequence
follows the activity the team presented to the domain specialists
(SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}).

G.3 was revised after the PSG was submitted. The submitted PSG said
Squigglr adjusts guidance and difficulty automatically. Following the
domain-specialist interview (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P2}{29:18})
and the Oct.~7 TA discussion (SRC-006), the team assigned \emph{lesson
selection, starting-stage selection and discretionary stage changes} to a
supporting adult (G.3). A passing drawing attempt still advances
automatically to the next stage \emph{within the lesson the adult selected}
(G.2, BR-002); completing a lesson never automatically selects another
lesson.

\emph{Purpose:} adults choose which lesson a child practises and may let

\subsection{Hands-On Users of the Project}

Squigglr has two groups of hands-on users, both primary stakeholders:
children, who complete drawing activities, and supporting adults, who choose activities and review
records.

\paragraph{Children.} Kindergarten and primary school learners, and older
children who need continued support, including children with autism, ADHD,
or other developmental or learning differences (SRC-001). The intended age
range is not yet defined; it is recorded as an open item, \texttt{AGE\_RANGE}
(TBD). Their characteristics vary widely, and a diagnosis alone does not
determine the support a child needs (SRC-001; \intref{Rayan}{2:04}).
The characteristics below matter most for the requirements, because they
shape how children read, draw and move through the application without
help:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reading ability:} some children cannot read yet, or may
  not understand written instructions. Squigglr has to communicate in ways
  that do not depend on reading, such as images and icons (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}) and spoken audio that can be muted (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{37:39}).
  \item \textbf{Motor skills:} fine-motor ability varies; some children find holding
  a stylus difficult, and grip can affect how they draw (SRC-005, \intref{P3}{25:36}). Fine-motor control is part of visual-motor practice, so children
  need to practise holding a tool, moving from larger tools to smaller
  ones closer to what schools use (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{26:46}). Squigglr
  therefore uses a stylus as its main input. Drawing with a finger is the
  easiest starting point (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{26:46}), and finger input is a
  stretch goal. Some children may also have gross-motor or vision
  challenges (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{19:05}).
  \item \textbf{Support needs:} once an adult starts a lesson, he should be
  able to practise without constant adult help (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}).
  He does not need to navigate, exit or restart a lesson. Adults decide when a child moves to a new lesson
  or skips a stage (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}).
  \item \textbf{Device experience:} many young children already know how
  to navigate touchscreen applications on tablets (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Supporting adults.} Parents, guardians, caregivers, educators,
and learning-support staff. They set up accounts, choose lessons and
starting stages, and review attempt records and progress summaries on a
tablet or computer (SRC-001; \intref{P1}{34:13}). Their technical
experience is assumed to be that of an everyday web user (assumption). They
need understandable information without a single overall score, diagnostic
labels, or
comparisons with other children (SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}).

\subsection{Personas}

The three personas below are \textbf{fictional}. They do not describe any
real child or adult. Personas are used because
requirements cannot be gathered from children directly, and the supervisor
recommended them for this case (SRC-004). There are at most three, and each is a short life story of a person. Details
that come from a source cite it; all other details were invented by the
team to make the persona concrete. No information was collected from children (SRC-004). Each
persona describes one individual; none represents all children or adults
in its group.

The personas describe people, not the product. They leave out design
choices such as screen layout or feedback wording, so they stay valid if
those choices change. Each persona has a fixed identifier (PER-1 to PER-3)
so that scenarios, use cases, usability tests and later documents can
refer to it. The three personas are linked: Adam is Sarah's son and one of
Janice's learners, so one scenario can follow him between home and school. Invented details should be checked with caregivers and educators when
the opportunity arises; if one changes, the scenarios that depend on it
must be reviewed.

\paragraph{PER-1 (learner): Adam.}
Adam is 15 years old; older children who need continued support are
in scope (SRC-001). He lives with his parents and no
siblings. Adam is autistic and has ADHD. He
loves Spider-Man, colours Spider-Man pictures at home, and tends to fixate
on things he enjoys.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Communication:} he is verbal but speaks in short, simple
  sentences, and most of his responses are memorized. He can say how he
  feels or what he wants, such as being hungry or wanting to play, but
  cannot hold the conversations typical for his age. He understands
  spoken words and instructions well.
  \item \textbf{Reading and writing:} he reads and writes only with a
  parent's support. He can read a few short words he recognizes, such as
  ``yes'' and ``no'', but not longer words or sentences.
  \item \textbf{Learning:} he learns best by watching someone do a task
  first and then copying it, rather than being told what to do without an
  example.
  \item \textbf{Attention:} he loses focus easily. For skill-building
  tasks such as reading or drawing, a caregiver needs to watch over him
  throughout. When he colours, which he enjoys, he can work alone for a
  while, but needs encouragement every now and then to stay focused and
  finish.
  \item \textbf{Frustrations:} he becomes agitated when a task is repeated
  over and over.
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology and tools:} he knows how to use an
  iPad. He has no experience with a stylus, but uses crayons, pens,
  pencils, coloured pencils and markers.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} finish a picture of something he likes and show
  it to his family.
  \item \textbf{Context of use:} at home with his mother nearby, and at
  school in sessions with Janice.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{PER-2 (caregiver): Sarah.}
Sarah is Adam's mother and works full time. She wants Adam
to keep building the drawing and fine-motor skills that support writing. She is not a specialist and learns about Adam's needs from
his teachers and therapists.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology:} an everyday web user who wants
  things to work without setup.
  \item \textbf{Goals:} let Adam practise without being called over
  constantly (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}); choose what Adam practises and decide
  when he moves on (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}).
  \item \textbf{Review habits:} looks through Adam's practice history on a
  laptop once a week to see which stages he reached and what keeps going
  wrong (SRC-001). Shares what she sees
  with Janice at parent meetings.
  \item \textbf{Concerns:} does not want Adam reduced to a single score, labelled or compared
  with other children (SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}); wants Adam's data kept
  private.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{PER-3 (educator): Janice.}
Janice is a teacher at a school for students with special needs who
works with several learners each day, including Adam. She
has a short slot with each learner and little time to set things up
between them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Environment:} the school's devices are managed centrally,

  \item \textbf{Attitude to technology:} comfortable with the school's web

  \item \textbf{Goals:} set a different activity for each learner

  sure one learner's records are never shown to another.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Solution Constraints}
The supervisor, the TA and the domain specialists set the form of the
solution.

\begin{description}
\item[CON-001] Squigglr shall be a website used in a web browser.\\
\emph{Rationale:} Installing apps on school devices is difficult, and for
Canadian schools a website is much easier to adopt than an app-store app.\\
\emph{Imposed by:} supervisor and TA.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} Squigglr opens and runs from a web address in a
browser.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-003, SRC-004.
\item[CON-002] Squigglr shall require no installation on the user's
device.\\
\emph{Rationale:} Shared school devices are often managed by the school,
so adults may not be able to install software. Opening a web address is
enough to start.\\
\emph{Imposed by:} supervisor and TA.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} a first-time user starts an activity without
installing any app, plug-in or browser extension.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-003, SRC-004.
\item[CON-003] Squigglr shall work on touchscreen devices with stylus
input.\\
\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr targets visual-motor skills, which include fine
motor control. Children build that control by practising with a held tool,
moving from large tools to smaller ones like pencils (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{26:46}).
Grip varies between children and affects how they draw (SRC-005, \intref{P3}{25:36}), so finger input is kept as a stretch goal (SRC-001).\\
\emph{Imposed by:} domain specialists, within the scope agreed with the
supervisor and TA.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} each activity can be completed with a stylus on a
touchscreen device; the device and browser combinations are TBD.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001, SRC-003, SRC-004, SRC-005.
\item[CON-007] Squigglr shall not select another lesson, skip a
guidance stage or move backward to an earlier stage unless a supporting
adult authorizes that change. Normal forward movement to the next stage
following a passing attempt \emph{within the selected lesson} is not an
unauthorized change (G.2, BR-002).\\
\emph{Rationale:} Choosing what a child is ready to practise requires
judgement by the adult who knows the child (SRC-005,
\intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P2}{29:18}). This restriction applies to
lesson selection and discretionary stage changes, not to the fixed
pass-based steps already defined for an adult-selected lesson. Squigglr
may suggest going back a stage, but an adult must accept (G.3).\\
\emph{Imposed by:} domain specialists' advice on adult choice, clarified
by the team's progression decision.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} test that passing a tracing or endpoint stage
advances within the current lesson without an additional adult action;
passing the final stage completes that lesson without starting another.
Verify that switching lessons, skipping and returning to an earlier stage
cannot occur without an adult choice or acceptance.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001, SRC-005 (\intref{P1}{28:37};
\intref{P2}{29:18}), SRC-006; team decision documented in
\href{https://github.com/AHMEDELZARIA/squigglr/issues/132}{\#132}.
\end{description}

\subsection{Symbolic Constants}
\label{sec:constants}
Table~\ref{tab:goalconst} specifies the parameters used in guided lessons,
creative refinement and progress-summary verification. The six
adult-configurable parameters have an allowed range and a default value
for each learner profile (BR-008, \srcref{008}). Their defaults are
the midpoints of their respective ranges. Each configuration shall satisfy
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} $>$ \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} $\geq$ \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}.
Drawing-stage results are expressed on a normalized scale from 0 to 100;
higher results indicate closer agreement with the stage's fixed criteria.
The result is not a measure of the child's ability (BR-006). Attempt-count
settings shall be integers, and values violating the allowed ranges or
the two cross-setting inequalities shall be rejected (BR-008).

\paragraph{Basis and verification of numerical values.}
The project team selected the initial ranges, defaults, five-point
\texttt{IMPROVE\_MARGIN} and 0--100 result scale in a team discussion on
15 October 2026 concerning issue
\href{https://github.com/AHMEDELZARIA/squigglr/issues/132}{\#132}.
These are team-selected engineering starting values, not thresholds
established by child testing or supplied numerically by the supervisor.
The supervisor's 14 October guidance (\srcref{008}) requires
adult-configurable ranges, midpoint defaults and example drawings
illustrating both ends of the \texttt{STAGE\_PASS} range. The chosen
values and the drawing-to-score interpretation are checked using
prepared or adult-made examples at the lower and upper boundaries of
each configurable range (OI-05; CON-005--006).

\emph{Default:} 8; must exceed \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}.
\emph{Trace:} G.3, BR-002, OI-05.\\
\addlinespace

\emph{Unit:} attempts. \emph{Range:} 1--3.
\emph{Default:} 2. \emph{Trace:} G.3, BR-002, OI-05.\\
\addlinespace

stage-result points across consecutive comparison windows that qualifies

\emph{Value:} 5 points (fixed by the team).
\emph{Trace:} G.3, BR-002, OI-05.\\

\section{Relevant Facts And Assumptions}
\label{sec:facts-assumptions}
This section records what is known to be true now, the rules that govern
how Squigglr may be used, and what the team believes but has not verified.
Facts and assumptions are kept apart so that a reader can see which
requirements rest on unconfirmed beliefs. The Hazard Analysis uses the
assumptions as possible causes of hazards: if an assumption turns out to
be false, the requirements that depend on it must be reviewed. Each entry identifies the goals, constraints or product behaviours it
supports; Section~\ref{sec:functional} specifies the corresponding
functional requirements.

\subsection{Relevant Facts}
\label{sec:facts}
Each fact below is true at the time of writing and is supported by a
source in Appendix~\ref{app:sources}. Facts from the interview are expert
statements, not observations of children.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[FACT-001] Visual-motor integration is linked to handwriting
legibility in kindergarten children, but the link is a correlation; it
does not show that practising one improves the other (SRC-001;
\intref{Rayan}{2:04}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} Squigglr makes no claim that its practice improves
handwriting or visual-motor skills (BR-006).
\item[FACT-002] The study of structured, graded practice cited in the
Problem Statement had no control group. It supports the idea of graded
practice but does not show that Squigglr will produce the same results
(SRC-001; \intref{Rayan}{2:04}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} effectiveness is not a goal or a fit criterion of this
project.
\item[FACT-003] Motor and handwriting difficulties occur in some autistic
children and some children with ADHD, with severity ranging from mild to
severe; not every child in these groups has them (SRC-001;
\intref{Rayan}{2:04}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} guidance and difficulty are set per child by an adult,
not per diagnosis (G.3).
\item[FACT-004] The specialists stated that deciding automatically when a
child is ready for the next level requires assessment, which is too
complex for the project's time frame, and that an adult who knows the
child should decide (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P2}{29:18}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for adult-controlled lesson selection and
nonroutine stage changes (CON-007, G.3, BR-003); the team retains fixed
pass-based forward progression within the selected lesson (G.2).
\item[FACT-005] The specialists stated that how a child holds a tool
affects how they draw, that finger input is the easiest start, and that
fine-motor practice needs a held tool, moving from large to small
(SRC-005, \intref{P3}{25:36}; \intref{P1}{26:46}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for stylus input (CON-003) with finger input as a
stretch goal.
\item[FACT-006] The specialists stated that tracing in the air in front
of a camera practises gross rather than fine motor skills, and that it
should come after the touchscreen activities when time is limited
(SRC-005, \intref{P1}{13:42}; \intref{P1}{16:13}; \intref{P2}{16:45}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} camera finger tracing stays a stretch goal.
\item[FACT-007] The specialists stated that young children may not read
yet but navigate games through images and icons, and that a child should
be able to enter a lesson, practise, restart and move back and forth
without constant adult help, while adults handle setup (SRC-005,
\intref{P1}{34:13}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} G.1 and the usability requirements; requirement IDs to
be linked when written.
\item[FACT-008] A study that involves children requires ethics approval;
meeting adult experts for feedback, without involving children, does not
(SRC-004; SRC-005, \intref{P2}{22:56}; \intref{P1}{33:00}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for CON-005 and CON-006.
\item[FACT-009] Installing applications on school-managed devices is
difficult, and a website is easier for Canadian schools to adopt (SRC-003,
SRC-004).\\
\emph{Relevance:} basis for CON-001 and CON-002.
\item[FACT-010] The specialists recommended starting from the Web Content
Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), which one participant stated are adopted
in law in many countries, and then applying design heuristics for autism
from the literature (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{39:26}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} accessibility and standards requirements; requirement
IDs to be linked when written.
\item[FACT-011] The course fixes the project's deadlines (CON-004,
SRC-002).\\
\emph{Relevance:} scope decisions, such as keeping assessment and camera
tracing out of the core product, follow from the time available.
\item[FACT-012] External requirements evidence includes a 43-minute
interview with three domain specialists and a follow-up questionnaire
answered by two of them. No parent, educator, school administrator or
occupational therapist has been consulted, and no child has taken part
in any source (SRC-005, SRC-009; \srcref{004}).\\
\emph{Relevance:} requirements based on what parents and educators need
rest on assumptions (ASM-006) until those groups are consulted.
\item[FACT-013] The interview did not cover several topics that affect
the requirements: how Squigglr compares with existing drawing apps (one
participant noted only that many exist (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{35:36})),
differences between home and school use, the
relative priority of the features, and how practice records should be
kept, shared or deleted (SRC-005).\\
The follow-up questionnaire later covered refinement in the creative space
and letter activities (SRC-009).\\
\emph{Relevance:} the requirements on these topics rest on assumptions
(ASM-010 to ASM-012) and are open to change.
\end{description}

\subsection{Business Rules}
\label{sec:business-rules}
The rules below govern who may do what with Squigglr and what Squigglr
must never do. They hold no matter how Squigglr is designed. Each rule has
an identifier of the form BR-\#\#\#, a rationale and its sources.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[BR-001] Only the adult who holds an account, a parent or an
educator, may create, view and manage the learner profiles under it and
their records. One account can hold several learner profiles. A learner
profile holds only the child's first name and a username that does not
identify the child; no other information about the child is collected.\\
\emph{Rationale:} practice records describe a child's difficulties and
are personal information. Keeping each profile to a first name and a
non-identifying username lets an adult tell children apart, for example on
a shared device, while collecting as little about them as possible. How
records are protected is a privacy requirement.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001, SRC-004; G.5, G.6.
\item[BR-002] Within an adult-selected lesson, Squigglr automatically
moves the child to the next guidance stage when an attempt meets the
current drawing stage's pass criteria (\texttt{STAGE\_PASS}). Passing
the independent drawing stage completes the lesson; it does not select or
start another lesson. After \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} (larger than
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}) unsuccessful attempts at one stage, the lesson
ends, and the furthest stage reached and the number of successful attempts
at each stage are saved. If, when \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} is reached, the
average result of the last \texttt{IMPROVE\_WINDOW} attempts is better
than that of the \texttt{IMPROVE\_WINDOW} attempts before them by at least
\texttt{IMPROVE\_MARGIN}, the child is improving and Squigglr grants a single extension of up to
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_EXTENSION} additional evaluable attempts. The extension
cannot be granted a second time at the same stage, regardless of later
result changes. Total evaluable attempts at a stage never exceed
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX}.\\
\emph{Rationale:} moving forward on a pass checks the drawing against fixed
criteria and does not assess the child (G.2). Ending the lesson stops a
child from repeating a stage they cannot pass without limit, and saving the
progress lets the adult decide what comes next. Extending the limit while
the child improves avoids cutting off practice that is working, and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} keeps the stop. The comparison uses only the
child's own attempts against the stage's fixed criteria, so it does not
assess the child.\\
\emph{Source:} G.2, G.3, G.5.
\item[BR-003] A supporting adult chooses which lesson the child practises
and its starting stage. Squigglr never changes lessons, skips a stage or
moves back a stage on its own. Passing an attempt may advance the child
to the next stage \emph{within that same lesson} as defined by BR-002. After
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} failed attempts in a row, Squigglr may
suggest going back a stage, unless the child is improving (BR-002); the change happens only if an adult accepts.\\
\emph{Rationale:} deciding readiness needs assessment that is out of
scope (FACT-004), and the adult knows the child's level.\\
\emph{Source:} CON-007; G.3; SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37};
\intref{P2}{29:18}.
\item[BR-004] A guided lesson record keeps the child's actual strokes.
Squigglr never replaces them with a corrected or ideal shape.\\
\emph{Rationale:} adults judge progress from the records; a record that
shows a corrected shape would mislead them.\\
\emph{Source:} G.5; SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}.
\item[BR-005] Refinement of strokes happens only in the creative space.
It is automatic and minimal, the refined drawing stays editable, and the
child's original drawing can be restored, by the child or, if the child
cannot, by the adult.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the work must remain the child's own, and refinement
must never reach the records adults rely on (BR-004). Refinement needs no
action from the child, and an adult can restore the original for a child
who cannot use the control.\\
\emph{Source:} G.4; SRC-001.
\item[BR-006] Squigglr gives no diagnosis, no judgement of a child's
abilities, no comparison with other children, and no claim that its
practice improves any skill.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the team are not occupational therapists and cannot
judge a child's abilities (SRC-008), and the evidence does not support
effectiveness claims (FACT-001, FACT-002).\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001, SRC-008; SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}.
\item[BR-007] Feedback during a guided activity comes from observations
the team defines in advance, such as a missed part of an outline. It is
not personalized advice generated by an AI model.\\
\emph{Rationale:} predefined observations can be reviewed and checked
before release; generated advice to a child cannot.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-001; SRC-005, \intref{Moustafa}{6:03}.
\item[BR-008] The supporting adult sets \texttt{STAGE\_PASS},
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}, \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT},
\texttt{IMPROVE\_WINDOW}, \texttt{ATTEMPT\_EXTENSION} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} for each child.
When an adult creates a child profile, Squigglr asks them to choose each
value, and for \texttt{STAGE\_PASS} shows example drawings of what its
minimum and maximum look like. The adult can change the values later. A value the adult does not
choose is set to the middle of its range. Squigglr accepts only values
within the range, only an \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} larger than
\texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}, and only an \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} at
least \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the adult knows the child, and a value that suits one
child may not suit another (FACT-003). The team sets only the minimum and
maximum, so no single value has to be shown right for every child.\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-008; CON-007; Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}.
\item[BR-009] The team sets the minimum and maximum of each adult-set
value, and the fixed values in Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}, using
adult-made drawings, never from testing with children.\\
\emph{Rationale:} no study with children takes place (CON-005, CON-006).\\
\emph{Source:} SRC-004, SRC-008; Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}.
\end{description}

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}
The assumptions below are beliefs the team holds but has not verified.
Each has an identifier of the form ASM-\#\#\#, a rationale, what depends
on it, and the effect if it is false. Assumptions marked as relevant to
safety are inputs to the Hazard Analysis.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[ASM-001] An adult is present when a child starts a guided lesson, to
choose the lesson and the starting stage.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists described adults handling setup and
deciding progression (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}; \intref{P1}{34:13}).
How often this happens at home or in class is unknown.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, BR-003, CON-007.\\
\emph{If false:} a child may start at a level an adult did not choose.
Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-002] A child may continue practising without an adult watching,
but an adult nearby remains responsible for the child's physical safety,
wellbeing and time on the device. Squigglr cannot observe or prevent
physical harm, distress or overuse.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists advised that children should practise
without constant adult help (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{34:13}), and the
Problem Statement expects adult support as each learner needs (SRC-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.1, the safety requirements (requirement IDs to be
linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} the safety requirements must cover unsupervised use,
for example limits on session length or sound. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-003] Homes and schools that use Squigglr have a touchscreen
device with a stylus that works in a supported browser.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the stylus is the main input (CON-003), and the
interview example used a tablet with a stylus (SRC-005,
\intref{Rayan}{24:56}). Which devices families and schools own is not
known. The supported device and browser combinations are TBD.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} CON-003, G.1, G.2.\\
\emph{If false:} children without a stylus can still use Squigglr with a
finger, but not as intended: the stage checks and value ranges are set for
stylus input, and tuning them for finger input is a stretch goal.
\item[ASM-004] Supported browsers report stylus strokes in enough detail
(position and order of points) for Squigglr to check an attempt against
\texttt{STAGE\_PASS} and to store the actual strokes.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the stage checks and the records both depend on the
strokes; this has not been tested on any device yet.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.2, G.5, BR-004.\\
\emph{If false:} an attempt may be judged wrongly or stored inaccurately,
which would mislead the adult. The team may then need to deliver Squigglr
as an installed app, if input there is more reliable, which would change
CON-001. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-005] A network connection is available during normal use, for
loading activities and for saving and viewing records. Offline use is out
of the initial scope.\\
\emph{Rationale:} stated in the Problem Statement (SRC-001); school and
home networks have not been checked.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.5, G.6, CON-001.\\
\emph{If false:} attempts may be lost when the connection drops, and an
adult may decide from incomplete records. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-006] The supporting adult can judge from the records when a
child should move on, stay or go back.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists placed this decision with a parent or
educator who knows the child (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{28:37}). Squigglr
shows the adult every value it records, such as each attempt's result and
the \texttt{STAGE\_PASS} and \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} in use for that
attempt, and the adult chooses which of them their view shows, so the team
does not have to pick which measures matter.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, G.6, CON-007, BR-003.\\
\emph{If false:} a child may be kept at a level that is too hard or too
easy; the adult views would need more guidance. Relevant to
safety.
\item[ASM-007] The adult who uses the adult views can read the language of
the interface. The interface language is TBD. Children do not need to
read to use the child views.\\
\emph{Rationale:} adult tasks such as reviewing records need text, while
child navigation uses images and icons (FACT-007).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.6 and the usability requirements (requirement IDs
to be linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} adults may misread records or settings.
\item[ASM-008] On a shared device, an adult makes sure the right child
is selected before a session and that another child cannot open the
previous child's records.\\
\emph{Rationale:} school devices may be shared by several children
(SRC-004). One adult account can hold several learner profiles (BR-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} BR-001, G.5.\\
\emph{If false:} attempts may be saved to the wrong child, or a child's
records may be seen by others. Relevant to safety and privacy.
\item[ASM-009] The adults who use Squigglr for a child have the right to
see that child's records, and any consent this needs is obtained outside
Squigglr.\\
\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr cannot verify a parent's or school's authority
over a child; it only knows the adult who holds the account (BR-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} BR-001, the privacy requirements (requirement IDs to
be linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} an unauthorised adult could see a child's records.
Relevant to privacy.
\item[ASM-010] Lines, basic shapes and simple drawings are suitable
practice content for the intended children, without letters.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists did not object to this content, and
nurseries focus on these skills (SRC-005, \intref{P1}{20:27}). Two
specialists saw letters as a later stage after shapes (SRC-009.04), so
they remain a stretch goal (SRC-001).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.1, G.2.\\
\emph{If false:} the activity set changes.
\item[ASM-011] Children and adults value automatic refinement in the
creative space, and children understand that the refined drawing is still
theirs.\\
\emph{Rationale:} questionnaire respondents supported subtle, optional
refinement that preserves the child's drawing (SRC-009.01), but their
responses do not establish children's or caregivers' preferences.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.4, BR-005.\\
\emph{If false:} refinement may discourage a child, and G.4 would need
to change.
\item[ASM-012] Squigglr serves the same needs at home and in school, so
one set of requirements covers both.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists named homes, kindergartens, schools and
rehabilitation centres as possible settings (SRC-005,
\intref{P2}{21:25}) but did not discuss differences between them
(FACT-013).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, G.6, BR-001.\\
\emph{If false:} separate requirements may be needed, for example for an
educator managing several children.
\item[ASM-013] Schools allow access to the Squigglr website on their
managed devices and networks.\\
\emph{Rationale:} a website is easier to adopt than an installed app
(FACT-009), but no school has confirmed that it would not block the site.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} CON-001, CON-002.\\
\emph{If false:} school use needs approval from administrators.
\item[ASM-014] Strokes drawn by adults, or prepared examples, are close
enough to children's drawing to verify stage checks and refinement.\\
\emph{Rationale:} evaluation uses adults only (CON-005, CON-006).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.2, G.4, BR-009, and the verification plan.\\
\emph{If false:} the range of \texttt{STAGE\_PASS} and the value of
\texttt{REFINE\_TOLERANCE} may be wrong for children, so attempts may be
judged wrongly. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-015] An adult can turn off audio feedback, or stop a session,
if sound or on-screen feedback upsets a child.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the specialists recommended that users can turn audio
off (SRC-005, \intref{P2}{37:39}). Children react to sensory feedback in
different ways, and the team has no information on which feedback upsets
which children.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} the accessibility and safety requirements
(requirement IDs to be linked when written).\\
\emph{If false:} feedback must be safe by default rather than relying on
an adult. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-016] A supporting adult can choose adult-set values
(Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}) that suit their child, helped by the example
drawings for \texttt{STAGE\_PASS}, or leave the defaults.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the supervisor advised leaving these values to the adult
(SRC-008); no adult has tried the choice yet.\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.2, G.3, BR-008.\\
\emph{If false:} a child may be held at a stage too long or moved on too
soon, which can frustrate them. Relevant to safety.
\item[ASM-017] A rise in the average result of a child's recent attempts
by at least \texttt{IMPROVE\_MARGIN} reflects real improvement, not chance
variation between drawings.\\
\emph{Rationale:} drawing results vary from one attempt to the next; the
team will check the margin with prepared adult-made sequences, not with
children (CON-005, CON-006).\\
\emph{Depends on it:} G.3, BR-002.\\
\emph{If false:} a lesson may be extended for a child who is not
improving, or not extended for one who is. \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX}
still ends the lesson. Relevant to safety.
\end{description}

\subsection{Learning Activity Requirements}
\label{sec:fr-learning}

This subsection specifies the externally observable behaviour of guided
lessons. It builds on G.1--G.3, CON-003, CON-007 and BR-002--BR-009,
with source statements in Appendix~\ref{app:sources}.
The core catalogue covers lines, shapes and simple drawings; letter tracing
is deferred (ASM-010, LC-001). Delivery-phase identifiers follow the
conventions in the Introduction and the phase plan in
Section~\ref{sec:tasks}.

\paragraph{Within-lesson progression and adult control.}
A supporting adult selects each lesson and its starting stage and authorizes
skips or backward changes (G.3, CON-007). Within the selected lesson, a
passing drawing attempt automatically advances to the next guidance stage
(G.2, BR-002). Passing the independent stage completes the current lesson;
Squigglr does not automatically select another lesson.

\subsection{Formal Specification}
\label{sec:fr-formal}

The most safety-relevant learning behaviour is formalized here as a
\emph{labelled transition system}: a set of states and named events with
rules describing when an event changes the state. The model specifies
externally observable outcomes rather than a particular user interface or
scoring algorithm. It covers guidance levels, attempt classification,
optional suggestions, automatic pass-based advancement within a lesson,
and the unsuccessful-attempt stopping rule.
Every variable used below has a type or domain; arrows indicate transitions
\emph{caused by the event above the arrow}, not automatic passage of time.
This notation applies only to guided learning activities.

\paragraph{Domains and state.}
For each selected lesson $\ell$ and learner profile $p$, let
\[
\begin{aligned}
L &= \{D,T,E,I\}, & A &= \{T,E,I\},\\
R &= \{\textsf{complete},\textsf{incomplete},\textsf{invalid}\},\\
M &= \{\textsf{assisted},\textsf{independent}\},\\
Q &= \{\textsf{active},\textsf{suggested},\textsf{ended}\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The levels $D,T,E,I$ mean \emph{demonstration}, full-outline
\emph{tracing}, \emph{endpoint-only} and \emph{independent} drawing,
respectively. Only levels in $A$ admit scored drawing attempts; $D$ is a
non-scored demonstration. A state is
\[
\sigma=(\ell,p,l,q,n_f,n_a,k,H,s,z,x,f),
\]
where $l\in L$ is the current guidance level of the adult-selected lesson,
$q\in Q$ is the session status, $n_f\in\mathbb{N}_0$ counts non-passing
evaluable attempts at this stage, $n_a\in\mathbb{N}_0$ counts finalized,
evaluable attempts at this stage, $k\in\mathbb{N}_0$ is the consecutive
non-passing streak, and $H=(r_1,\ldots,r_m)$ is the ordered list of
stage-result values. The flag $s\in\{0,1\}$ indicates a pending
suggestion; $z\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether the single stage extension
has been granted; $x\in\mathbb{N}_0$ counts evaluable attempts consumed
\emph{after} that grant, with $0\le x\le e_p$ and $x=0$ when $z=0$.
Finally, $f\in L$ is the furthest stage reached in the lesson.
On a stage change (either normal pass-based progression or an
authorized adult adjustment), the stage-specific counts, streak, history,
pending suggestion and extension state reset to zero or empty; $f$ retains
the furthest stage reached so far. \emph{No child ability estimate} is
part of this state (BR-006).

\paragraph{Drawing input and attempt classification.}
An attempt is a finite ordered list of stylus strokes
$X=(x_1,\ldots,x_j)$, where every $x_i$ is an ordered list of
reported points. Lifting the stylus closes one $x_i$, not the attempt.
The terminal event $\operatorname{Finalize}(X)$ denotes the end of input
collection for an attempt. This abstract event does not prescribe a
particular user-interface control. A stylus lift alone does not finalize
the attempt; interruption before finalization cannot produce a passed
result (FR-LRN-008--010).

Let $\operatorname{Valid}_{\ell,l}(X)$ mean that all input required to
interpret the strokes is available, and let
$\operatorname{Complete}_{\ell,l}(X)$ mean that every required part of
the drawing is present according to the lesson's fixed stage criteria.
These functions use the fixed completeness criteria of the selected lesson
and stage. Stage-result values use the normalized 0--100 scale specified in
Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}; the actual scoring function is defined for
each lesson's drawing criteria rather than as an assessment of a learner.
Define
\[
C_{\ell,l}(X)=
\begin{cases}
\textsf{invalid} & \neg\operatorname{Valid}_{\ell,l}(X),\\
\textsf{complete} & \operatorname{Valid}_{\ell,l}(X)\land
                      \operatorname{Complete}_{\ell,l}(X),\\
\textsf{incomplete} & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
The guidance classification uses the level of the actual attempt:
\[
G(l)=\begin{cases}
\textsf{assisted} & l\in\{T,E\},\\
\textsf{independent} & l=I.
\end{cases}
\]
Let $v_{\ell,l}(X)\in[0,100]$ be the normalized stage result of an
evaluable drawing. Its threshold is
$\theta_p=\texttt{STAGE\_\allowbreak PASS}(p)$, taken from the adult's approved
profile value (or midpoint default under BR-008). The pass predicate is
\[
\operatorname{Pass}(X,l,p)\iff
C_{\ell,l}(X)=\textsf{complete}
\land v_{\ell,l}(X)\ge\theta_p.
\]
A missing, malformed or interrupted attempt never satisfies this
predicate. Non-passing but evaluable attempts contribute to the
unsuccessful-attempt count. Invalid or interrupted input is never interpreted as success and does
not contribute to a stage's evaluated-attempt or unsuccessful-attempt
counters. The presented feedback is selected from the fixed set of
observations in BR-007 rather than generated advice.

\paragraph{Improvement and suggestion predicates.}
Use the constants defined in Section~\ref{sec:constants}:
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_p &= \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_\allowbreak STREAK}(p), &
 b_p &= \texttt{ATTEMPT\_\allowbreak LIMIT}(p),\\
w_p &= \texttt{IMPROVE\_\allowbreak WINDOW}(p), &
 e_p &= \texttt{ATTEMPT\_\allowbreak EXTENSION}(p),\\
 h_p &= \texttt{ATTEMPT\_\allowbreak LIMIT\_\allowbreak MAX}(p), &
 \delta &= \texttt{IMPROVE\_\allowbreak MARGIN}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For $H=(r_1,\ldots,r_m)$, define the total improvement predicate
\[
\operatorname{Improving}(H,p)=
\begin{cases}
\textsf{false}, & m<2w_p,\\
\displaystyle
\left(\frac{1}{w_p}\sum_{i=m-w_p+1}^{m} r_i
-\frac{1}{w_p}\sum_{i=m-2w_p+1}^{m-w_p}r_i\ge\delta\right),
& m\ge 2w_p.
\end{cases}
\]
Insufficient history therefore does not count as improvement and does
not delay a difficulty suggestion. For the default $w_p=2$, the first
comparison uses four evaluable attempts; a shorter history still allows
the suggestion at the selected failure-streak threshold.

For an eligible return to a preceding guidance level, the suggestion
trigger is
\[
\operatorname{SuggestBack}(\sigma)\iff
q=\textsf{active}\land s=0\land l\in\{E,I\}\land k\ge d_p
\land\neg\operatorname{Improving}(H,p).
\]
The rule applies to drawing levels $E$ and $I$; $T$ has no preceding
drawing level. A suggestion changes $q$ to $\textsf{suggested}$ and $s$
to $1$, but \emph{does not change $l$}. Declining or ignoring a suggestion
resets the streak counter $k$ to zero, preventing an immediate re-offer;
a new suggestion requires another $d_p$ consecutive failures.

\paragraph{State-transition rules.}
Table~\ref{tab:lrn-transitions} gives the level-changing and
stage-stopping rules. Here $\sigma'$ is the state after the event,
``unchanged'' means the active lesson and level remain exactly as before,
and $\operatorname{AdultAccept}$ is a verified adult action rather than any
child drawing input. Likewise, $\operatorname{AdultSelect}$ is the
authorized action selecting a lesson and its starting level.
A granted extension sets $z=1$ once, with $x=0$; each subsequently
finalized evaluable attempt increments $x$. The extension cannot be
granted again at this stage even if later scores qualify as improvement.
After finalization, a passing result takes priority over stop and
suggestion rules. Otherwise, reaching the base failure limit first
triggers either the single extension grant (if improving and still below
the hard cap) or lesson end. After a grant, exhausting $x=e_p$ or
reaching $n_a=h_p$ ends the lesson. A suggestion is offered only when
none of these higher-priority rules ends the lesson.
For pass-based forward transitions define
$\operatorname{Next}(T)=E$ and $\operatorname{Next}(E)=I$; $I$ has no
successor within its lesson. Passing the last stage ends \emph{that} lesson.

\paragraph{Invariants and verification obligations.}
For every reachable session state and every adult- or learner-generated
event, the model requires:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\textbf{I1:}\quad \ell'\ne\ell
  \Longrightarrow\operatorname{AdultSelect};\\
&\textbf{I2:}\quad \text{suggestion ignored or declined}
  \Longrightarrow (\ell',l')=(\ell,l);\\
&\textbf{I3:}\quad C_{\ell,l}(X)\ne\textsf{complete}
  \Longrightarrow \neg\operatorname{Pass}(X,l,p);\\
&\textbf{I4:}\quad \text{pass/feedback never substitutes refined strokes
   for actual guided strokes};\\
&\textbf{I5:}\quad n_a\le h_p\ \land\ 0\le x\le e_p
   \land (z=0\Rightarrow x=0);\\
&\textbf{I6:}\quad \operatorname{Pass}(X,T,p)
  \Longrightarrow (\ell',l')=(\ell,E);\\
&\textbf{I7:}\quad \operatorname{Pass}(X,E,p)
  \Longrightarrow (\ell',l')=(\ell,I);\\
&\textbf{I8:}\quad \operatorname{Pass}(X,I,p)
  \Longrightarrow q'=\textsf{ended}\land\ell'=\ell.
\end{aligned}
\]
Outside ordinary pass-based progression and the demonstration-to-practice
transition, a change to the active stage requires an authorized adult
selection or acceptance (CON-007). Invariants I1 and I6--I8 ensure that
a new lesson is never selected automatically and that passing an attempt
advances only within the current lesson. I2--I5 cover suggestions, invalid
input, faithful records, bounded stage attempts and the one-time extension.
These rules are verified using prepared adult-made attempt sequences
(CON-005--006), including threshold cases with insufficient improvement
history, declined suggestions, a full extension and the hard cap in
Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}.

\begin{description}
\item[SAF-RAY-001] Squigglr shall allow lesson choice, starting-stage

\emph{Fit criterion:} in prepared walkthroughs, no adult-only action
completes without passing the adult check, and a pending suggestion changes
nothing until an adult acts.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-001, H-RAY-006.
\item[SAF-RAY-002] Before a lesson starts, Squigglr shall check the device
and browser against \texttt{SUPPORTED\_DEVICES} (TBD) and, if they do not
match, show an adult-readable notice that results may be unreliable.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the team does not control the device (CON-001--CON-003).\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} on each prepared unsupported combination the notice
appears before the first attempt; on supported ones it does not.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-002.
\item[SAF-RAY-003] Squigglr shall keep each completed attempt on the device
until the server confirms it is saved, and shall tell the adult which
attempts could not be saved.\\
\emph{Rationale:} offline access is not promised (SRC-005,
\intref{Moustafa}{6:03}), and G.5 needs complete records.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} with the connection cut during or after prepared
attempts, no confirmed attempt is lost and every unsaved attempt is reported
to the adult.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-002.
\item[SAF-RAY-004] No record, summary or export shall show a
diagnostic label, a judgement of the child's abilities or a comparison with
other children, and every summary and
export shall state that it describes practice only and is not an assessment
or diagnosis.\\
\emph{Rationale:} Squigglr makes no diagnosis (SRC-001; SRC-005,
\intref{Moustafa}{6:03}), and adults reading records may have no training.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} inspection of every summary and export template finds
none of the excluded content and finds the statement present.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-003.
\item[SAF-RAY-005] Squigglr shall end a lesson at \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT},
or at \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} when extended, without any action from
the child, and shall keep the progress saved up to that point.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the child is not expected to exit a lesson, and no adult
may be watching (CA-001), so repeated failure must end on its own (G.3).\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} in prepared sequences of failed attempts, each lesson
ends at the limit in use with no child input, and the saved record matches
the attempts made.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-004.
\item[SAF-RAY-006] Squigglr shall record each lesson that ends at
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} or \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX}, and each
improvement extension, show the adult how many times these happened for
each lesson, and keep earlier attempts when a lesson is restarted.\\
\emph{Rationale:} a restart could otherwise hide repeated failure from the
adult.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} in prepared sequences that reach the limit and then
restart, the count and all earlier attempts appear in the adult view.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-005.
\item[SAF-RAY-007] Squigglr shall identify the active learner on every
practice screen in a form a child who cannot read can recognize, and shall
save every attempt and suggestion to that learner only.\\
\emph{Rationale:} school devices may be shared by several children
(SRC-004).\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} in prepared multi-learner sessions on one device,
every attempt and suggestion is saved under the learner shown when it was
made.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-006.
\item[SAF-RAY-008] Squigglr shall save a child's adult-set values
(Table~\ref{tab:goalconst}) only within their team-set ranges, with
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} larger than \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK} and
\texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} at least \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT}; shall use the middle of each range when the
adult does not choose; shall allow changes only after the adult check; and
shall show the values in use alongside the child's records.\\
\emph{Rationale:} the supervisor advised that the adult chooses these
values (SRC-008); a wrong value can stop a child passing a stage they can
draw, and a child must not change it.\\
\emph{Fit criterion:} in prepared walkthroughs, every out-of-range value and
every \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} not larger than \texttt{DIFFICULTY\_STREAK}
and every \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT\_MAX} below \texttt{ATTEMPT\_LIMIT} is
refused, an unset value equals the midpoint, and no change completes
without the adult check.\\
\emph{Hazards:} H-RAY-007.

\section{Appendix --- Reflection}
\label{app:reflection}

\input{../Reflection.text}

\begin{enumerate}

\item What went well while writing this deliverable?

\textbf{Rayan:} What went well was that most of my sections could be built on
what our stakeholders had actually told us, rather than on our own guesses.
Moustafa and I interviewed three domain specialists on October 1, 2026, and
the de-identified transcript is now an appendix to the SRS. Because of this,
Sections 1, 2 and 5 and the requirement sources in Appendix A cite the
specialists (P1 to P3) by timestamp, so a reader can check exactly where each
statement came from. Our meetings with our supervisor, Dr.~Anand, on September
23 and October 14, 2026, and with our TA on September 23 and October 7, 2026,
gave us the same kind of traceable source for the constraints and business
rules. It also helped that I split my part of the SRS into one issue and one
pull request per section, which kept each review small and let each section be
checked and merged on its own. The main thing I took from this is that
recording sources as we go makes the requirements much easier to justify and
to change later.

\item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how did
you resolve them?

\textbf{Rayan:} The main pain point was that decisions kept changing as we
learned more, and each change affected several sections at once. In our
interview on October 1, 2026, the specialists said that the supporting adult,
not the app, should decide when a child moves to an easier or harder stage, so
we changed our progression goal from automatic to adult-controlled. In our
meeting on October 14, 2026, Dr.~Anand advised that thresholds such as the
pass criteria and the number of failed attempts should be set by the adult for
each child within a range the team defines, which changed several constraints,
business rules and goals. The answers to the follow-up questionnaire that
Moustafa and I sent the specialists, returned between October 2 and 4, 2026,
then confirmed that letters belong in a later stage after shapes, so we kept
them as a stretch goal and recorded the order of stages as a likely change. I resolved
this by updating every affected section in the same pull request as each
change, and by recording anything still unsettled in Open Issues (Section 18),
so that nothing was left half-changed. A smaller problem was that several of
my branches were open at once and each added a row to the revision history, so
later pull requests conflicted on that table; I merged main into each branch
and kept every row. I also found that some early drafts included details
nobody had actually discussed, so I went back through each section and removed
any claim I could not trace to a source or a team decision. Looking back, I
learned that requirements are never really finished early in a project, and
that keeping each change in one place and tracing it to its source is what
makes them manageable.

\item How many of your requirements were inspired by speaking to your
client(s) or their proxies (e.g. your peers, stakeholders, potential users)?

\textbf{Rayan:} In my sections, I wrote 24 requirements: 7 mandated
constraints, 9 business rules and 8 safety requirements. Of these, 18 cite at
least one conversation with our stakeholders or their proxies. Ten come from
our interview with the specialists on October 1, 2026, including that adults
choose the lesson and stage, that guided lesson records keep the child's
actual strokes, and that Squigglr gives no diagnosis. Eleven come from our
meetings with Dr.~Anand on September 23 and October 14, 2026, including that
Squigglr is a website, that no information is gathered from or about children,
and that the adult sets each child's thresholds within a team-set range. Some
cite both. The other six come from our own project documents and the course
requirements, such as the deliverable deadlines. Since none of these
stakeholders are the children themselves, nearly every requirement that
affects how a child uses Squigglr rests on what adults and specialists told
us, which is why we plan to check them with the domain specialists again later.

\item Which of the courses you have taken, or are currently taking, will help
your team to be successful with your capstone project.

\textbf{Rayan:} Two courses help directly. The first is SFWRENG 4HC3,
Human-Computer Interaction, which I am taking now and where our group's term
project is Squigglr itself. The user research we do there, such as planning
interviews with caregivers and educators and building personas, feeds straight
into the stakeholders and requirements in this document. The second is
SFWRENG 3RA3, Software Requirements, which I took in my third year. The
requirements document we wrote for that course followed Meyer's template
rather than Volere, but the concepts carry over directly. In 3RA3 we learned
to separate hard goals, which need a clear, measurable and verifiable
satisfaction criterion, from soft goals, which have to be broken down until
each part can be measured. That is the same idea as the fit criterion Volere
attaches to each requirement. We also learned to check every requirement
against three qualities, that it is necessary, verifiable and attainable,
and to write requirements with the EARS sentence patterns, such as ``When an
event occurs, the system shall\ldots'', so that the wording stays precise.
Being able to transfer those concepts helped me a lot with this deliverable in
particular, because I already knew how to write goals and requirements that
are precise and checkable and only had to adapt them to a new template.

\item What knowledge and skills will the team collectively need to acquire to
successfully complete this capstone project? Examples of possible knowledge
to acquire include domain specific knowledge from the domain of your
application, or software engineering knowledge, mechatronics knowledge or
computer science knowledge. Skills may be related to technology, or writing,
or presentation, or team management, etc. You should look to identify at
least one item for each team member.

\textbf{Rayan:} The most important knowledge the team needs is domain
knowledge about the children Squigglr is for: how autistic children and
children with ADHD develop visual-motor skills, how they use apps, and what
helps or frustrates them. None of us has a background in this area, and it is
not something we could safely guess, because a wrong assumption could make
the app harder or more upsetting for the children it is meant to help. That
is why it was necessary to get input from people who have designed software
for autistic children and children with ADHD, rather than relying only on our
own ideas. Their experience is what turned our general idea into requirements
we can actually justify.

\item For each of the knowledge areas and skills identified in the previous
question, what are at least two approaches to acquiring the knowledge or
mastering the skill? Of the identified approaches, which will each team
member pursue, and why did they make this choice?

\textbf{Rayan:} The two approaches we chose for acquiring this domain
knowledge were interviews and questionnaires with domain specialists.
Moustafa and I pursued both. We interviewed three specialists on October 1,
2026, because a live conversation let us ask follow-up questions and explore
topics we had not expected, such as who should control a child's
progression. We then sent them a written questionnaire, answered between
October 2 and 4, 2026, to cover the topics the interview did not reach, such
as refinement in the creative space and letter activities. The questionnaire
also gave the specialists time to think through their answers. Using both
worked well together: the interview showed us what we did not know, and the
questionnaire filled in the gaps it left.
